# Public Fixed Income — AI concentration

## Conclusion

AI concentration in public fixed income is already material, but there is no defensible single percentage for the whole asset class. The most decision-useful current measures are **6.2% of the US high-grade corporate market for a narrow, direct AI-buildout cohort** and **2.75–3.0% of the US high-yield index for high-performance-computing (HPC) issuers**. A broader high-grade ecosystem measure reaches **15.5%**, but it includes utilities and industrial suppliers whose debt is only partly exposed to AI and should therefore be treated as a common-factor risk envelope, not as 15.5% of bonds whose proceeds are wholly AI-related. The five largest hyperscalers alone are **4.15% of the J.P. Morgan US Liquid Index (JULI)**.

The clearest warning comes from issuance rather than the existing stock. In 2026 through mid-August, tracked global corporate and syndicated AI-linked financing was approximately **$417bn**, versus **$189bn in all of 2025**. Morgan Stanley's own decomposition is **~$267bn of US investment grade** across hyperscalers, data-centre construction, semiconductors and REITs, plus **$62bn non-USD equivalent**, with the balance in high yield and Section 4(a)(2) chip financing — so a substantial part of the headline is not eligible for a USD public bond index at all. HPC issuers represented **$31.1bn, or 40.4%, of non-refinancing US high-yield issuance** through mid-June despite being less than 3% of the index. These figures are not contradictory: they use different universes and show how quickly a large financing flow is beginning to change a much larger outstanding debt stock. [Morgan Stanley, *A Break from the Heat—For Now*, pp. 1–6](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Credit_Strategy_A_Break_from_the_Heat_For_Now-2026-08-14.pdf>); [J.P. Morgan, *AI Capex 2.0*, pp. 32–41](<research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf>).

On a transparent scenario basis, the narrow high-grade buildout sleeve reaches **approximately 11% by end-2030, within a 9.5–13% range**, and the broad ecosystem could exceed **20%**. HPC high yield reaches **approximately 8.5%, within a 6–12% range**, where the dominant uncertainty is not amortisation but the rate at which stabilised project debt graduates into investment grade and ABS/CMBS. Every forward figure is triangulated across three independent published routes — a capacity ceiling on which Morgan Stanley and Apollo independently converge, a capex bottom-up, and a decomposed issuance forecast — with no judgement retention parameter (§3). These are planning ranges, not point forecasts.

A separate stress case deserves board-level attention. AI issuers were roughly **one third of 2026 net investment-grade issuance**, a pace that is only compatible with Apollo's published sub-$1tn absorption ceiling if AI net issuance **halves from 2027**. Should the 2026 run-rate simply persist, the narrow high-grade sleeve reaches **15–16%** by end-2030, above every published anchor. AI share of net investment-grade issuance is therefore the highest-value single indicator on the monitoring dashboard (§3.3).

## 1. What concentration means and where it stands today

For credit, AI exposure should mean **the market value of debt whose repayment, spread or refinancing risk is materially linked to the AI investment cycle**. It has three distinct channels:

1. **Direct buildout:** hyperscalers, data-centre developers, semiconductor firms and HPC/neocloud borrowers raising debt to fund compute capacity.
2. **Enabling ecosystem:** utilities, power and electrical equipment, cooling, networking and other suppliers whose credit profile is increasingly tied to AI-related orders or contracted load.
3. **Disruption exposure:** incumbent software and services borrowers whose cash flows may be weakened by AI. This is a negative exposure and must not be added to buildout financing as though it were the same risk.

Concentration should be reported separately by rating and legal wrapper. An unsecured hyperscaler bond, an HPC secured bond, a data-centre ABS tranche and an SPV construction bond can share the same tenant or capex driver, but have different default, duration and collateral risks.

- The Bank of England's **3% at end-2025** for the same five hyperscalers is USD investment-grade debt on an earlier date and a different benchmark. Its finding that they exceeded **15% of early-2026 issuance** is a flow measure. - J.P. Morgan's **$165bn** of 2026 high-grade issuance covers the narrow buildout cohort through mid-June. The latter's rounded components sum to about $415bn; the $2bn difference is rounding.
- The **40.4% HY flow share** is `$31.1bn / $76.9bn` of non-refinancing issuance, not 40% of the outstanding HY market. It should be viewed as a leading indicator of future stock concentration.

## 2. Recommended measurement framework

The most defensible approach is a **stock–flow–look-through framework**, maintained separately for investment grade, high yield, loans and securitized credit.

### Stock

For each benchmark (B), measure the full market value of identified bonds rather than applying a generic revenue haircut:

$$
C^{stock}_{B,t}=\frac{\sum_i MV_{i,t}\times A_{i,t}}{\sum_{j\in B}MV_{j,t}},
$$

where (A=1) for directly classified buildout securities and (A\in[0,1]) only where an enabling issuer's AI-attributable exposure can be supported by a contract, customer, load, capacity or use-of-proceeds measure. A bond is a claim on the whole obligor; an arbitrary 25% or 50% haircut based on AI revenue would therefore misstate direct credit exposure. Where no defensible attribution exists, report the issuer in the broad envelope and mark the exposure unknown rather than assigning a precise score.

### Flow

Measure new borrowing over an identical currency, rating, instrument and time universe:

$$
C^{flow}_{B,t}=\frac{\text{AI-linked gross or net issuance in }B}{\text{total gross or net issuance in }B}.
$$

Gross and non-refinancing flow must not be mixed. Report the **flow–stock gap**, (C^{flow}-C^{stock}), as a pressure indicator; do not call the flow share the current portfolio weight.

### Look-through and risk

Map issuer, parent, SPV, guarantor, tenant/offtaker, collateral, currency, rating and maturity. Report both legal-obligor and ultimate-economic-counterparty concentration, because at least **$53bn of investment-grade data-centre construction bonds** was outside standard indices and hyperscaler leases/purchase commitments can generate third-party debt. This mapping should also prevent double counting when construction loans refinance into ABS/CMBS or when HY issuers migrate to IG. [Morgan Stanley, *Data Center/AI Financing: Investor Feedback*, pp. 3–16](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Corporate_and_Securitized_Credit_Data_Center_AI_Financing_Investor_Feedback-2026-07-13.pdf>).

Market-value concentration should be supplemented by spread duration, expected loss, secured/unsecured status, maturity walls and top-tenant share. The result is both an exposure weight and a measure of sensitivity to a common AI-capex shock; the two are not interchangeable.

## 3. Forward concentration

### 3.0 Method: one identity, no free parameters

Every forward number below comes from a single identity applied to a named benchmark:

$$
W_{2030}=\frac{S_{0}+N}{\left(D_{0}-S_{0}\right)\left(1+g\right)^{h}+S_{0}+N}
$$

where $D_0$ is the current benchmark market value, $S_0 = W_0 \times D_0$ is the current AI stock, $N$ is **net new AI debt retained in that benchmark by end-2030**, $g$ is the growth rate of the **non-AI** remainder, and $h = 4.5$ years from 30 June 2026.

Three properties matter for review:

1. **$N$ enters numerator and denominator.** New bonds join the index, so they raise the AI weight and the index simultaneously. Any construction that adds AI debt only to the numerator overstates the result.
2. **$g$ applies to the non-AI remainder only.** AI issuance was roughly one third of 2026 net investment-grade issuance ([Apollo, *2026 Midyear Credit Outlook*, p. 18, citing Goldman Sachs](<research/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf>)), so AI supply *is* index growth. Growing the whole index at $g$ and then adding AI double-counts.
3. **$N$ must be denominated in the same units as $D_0$** — net, USD, index-eligible.

Point 3 is the discipline the previous version of this section lacked. It applied a judgement "net retention" factor of 20–40% to J.P. Morgan's $2.1tn gross, global, all-wrapper borrowing forecast, and that single unsourced parameter drove the entire published range. It has been removed. Where a route still requires an eligibility conversion, the conversion is decomposed and sourced (Route C).

A prior-version diagnostic: the earlier scenario implied that only 40–60% of high-yield HPC issuance would remain in the index. Run forward just six months, it produced 3.3–3.8% at end-2026 against J.P. Morgan's published 4.5% waypoint — its own high case undershot. The identity is calibrated to that waypoint instead (§3.2).

#### Cross-checks, and one that does not reconcile

#### Two capacity families, which must not be mixed

Published "AI credit capacity" numbers fall into two groups that answer different questions. Mixing them is the same denominator error as comparing a market to an index, and it is why the figures appear to disagree when they do not.

| Family | Question answered | Published estimates |
|---|---|---|
| **Cohort-wide** — whole AI cohort against a sector-share ceiling | How much AI debt can the index hold before the sector looks historically extreme? | MS $898–935bn (rescaled); Apollo **< $1,000bn**. **This is Route A.** |
| **Single-name** — hyperscalers against issuer-size or ratings limits | How much can these specific borrowers issue before they hit a limit? | Goldman **~$510bn** (hyperscalers to the largest-US-bank level); MS **~$400bn** (leverage inside the current ratings band); Apollo's 8.8% path implies **~$535bn**; MS **$796bn** (each hyperscaler to the 2.6% average largest-issuer share) |

Three of the four single-name estimates cluster at **$400–535bn**, materially below Morgan Stanley's $796bn. Goldman's framing is the sharpest: hyperscalers have roughly **$2tn** of incremental borrowing capacity while remaining investment grade, but the US investment-grade market can absorb only about **$510bn** before issuer-concentration conventions bind — because the three largest issuers in the index are US banks, none with more than **$175bn** of index-eligible debt or more than **2.3%** of the index. Apollo's structure is identical from a different starting point: more than $2tn the ecosystem could support, under $1tn that public IG can absorb. **Two houses independently separate borrowing capacity from absorption capacity, and both put absorption at roughly a quarter to a half of capacity.**

Only the cohort-wide family is a valid input to the identity, because only it shares the model's denominator. The single-name family is a *constraint check* on whether the cohort-wide number is reachable by the issuers who would have to supply it.

This has a direct consequence for Route A. If hyperscalers can supply only ~$400–535bn of the $898–1,000bn cohort-wide band, then **$390–490bn must come from data-centre, semiconductor and REIT issuers rather than the hyperscalers themselves.** J.P. Morgan's forecast of ~$170bn a year of data-centre and semiconductor structures is consistent with that, but only if a meaningful share proves index-eligible — which is the same unknown as Route C's eligibility factor. The two constraints are not independent, and they bind on the same uncertainty.

**The single-name check also surfaces a ratings tension.** Morgan Stanley estimates "~$400bn of high-quality hyperscaler debt capacity while keeping leverage ratio consistent with current ratings' band" ([p. 1](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Credit_Strategy_A_Break_from_the_Heat_For_Now-2026-08-14.pdf>)), incorporating ~$585bn of S&P debt adjustments for leases, PPAs and guarantees projected for 2027. Apollo's 8.8% hyperscaler path requires roughly **$535bn** of net new hyperscaler debt. The two are compatible only if hyperscalers accept ratings migration within investment grade, if the S&P adjustments prove smaller, or if more of the buildout is financed by data-centre, semiconductor and REIT issuers rather than the hyperscalers themselves. **Reported leverage is not the leverage that binds**, and this is the mechanism by which a benign-looking index weight can still carry ratings risk.

> **Verification tier.** The Goldman figures come from *How AI Debt Is Reshaping Credit Markets* (Goldman Sachs Exchanges). The primary page returns HTTP 403 from this workspace and the two Goldman PDFs held in `research/` do not cover investment-grade credit, so these figures are **corroborated through secondary reporting rather than read from the primary source**. They are used for framing and as a single-name constraint check; they are **not** model inputs. Obtaining the primary transcript is listed in §3.5.

> **An open denominator question, and a test for it.** Goldman's "no issuer above $175bn or 2.3% of the index" implies an index base of roughly **$7.2–7.6tn**, and Morgan Stanley's capacity exhibit implies **$7.15tn**. Both sit well below the $9.4tn C0A0 read used as $D_0$. Apollo's Exhibit 27 also shows the largest issuer at 2.3%. The test is one line of client data: **if the largest single issuer in C0A0 weighs ~2.3%, the universes reconcile and the rescaling in this section holds; if it weighs ~1.9%, C0A0 is broader than the index these houses quote, and the cohort-wide rescaling to $898–935bn is overstated by roughly the same ratio.** This is the highest-priority open item in the section.

Directionally the houses agree. Morgan Stanley's implied ceiling of 14.2–14.5% sector share sits **above** this section's high case, so the scenario is not capacity-constrained on either house's numbers. Apollo separately estimates the wider AI ecosystem could support **more than $2tn** of additional IG debt, of which public IG absorbs under half — the residual being migration into private IG, ABS/CMBS and project structures that §2's look-through framework is designed to catch.

Apollo's superseded 2026 outlook projected the five hyperscalers rising from 3.0% at end-2025 to 6.8% by 2030 on a 20%-of-capex issuance assumption. **That figure should no longer be cited**: the same house's August 2026 update raises the path to 8.8% by 2030, and the cohort had already reached 4.15% in JULI by June 2026. Where the earlier version of this section concluded that "6–8% remains a reasonable 2030 core-obligor range", the refreshed number sits above it.

#### The broad ecosystem

The 15.5% wide sleeve is not modelled with the identity above, because it has no published constituent file and no separate issuance forecast. J.P. Morgan's earlier 75-issuer ecosystem work projected a move from 14.5% to **more than 20% by 2030**, and the June 2026 update raised the current broad measure to 15.5% and the five-year borrowing forecast from $1.5tn to $2.1tn. **More than 20% therefore remains a plausible broad-factor envelope, not a security-level forecast**, and it should continue to be reported as a common-factor risk boundary rather than as an exposure weight. #### Calibration to J.P. Morgan's own waypoint

Unlike investment grade, high yield has a published one-year checkpoint that can be back-tested. Applying the identity to 2H26 gross high-yield HPC issuance of $33.9bn (`$65bn − $31.1bn`):

| Retention on 2H26 issuance | Implied end-2026 weight |
|---:|---:|
| 25% | 3.27% |
| 50% | 3.82% |
| **75%** | **4.37%** |
| **80%** | **4.48%** ← matches J.P. Morgan's 4.5% |
| 100% | 4.91% |

Corroborated by the observed move: HPC rose from 1.07% at end-2025 to 2.75% by mid-June on $31.1bn of issuance — a stock increase of roughly $24.8bn, or **~80% conversion**. Corroborated again by Apollo, which puts direct data-centre exposure in high yield at **"nearly 5% of the index"** in August, already at J.P. Morgan's year-end waypoint ([Apollo, p. 18](<research/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf>)).

*Two caveats, both pushing the observed conversion rate upward:* Apollo's "direct data centre" cut is not identical to J.P. Morgan's HPC subsector, and part of the 1.07% → 2.75% move reflects issuers newly classified into the subsector rather than pure retention of flow. The calibration should therefore be read as 75–80% being consistent with three independent observations, not as a precisely measured rate.

**The prior version's stated rationale for a low retention was a category error.** It cited "average HPC bond duration is below 3.5 years" as grounds for rapid index attrition. That figure is *duration*, quoted by J.P. Morgan alongside spread-to-worst and therefore measured to call. Bonds leave an index at **maturity**, not at duration. A high-yield bond with a 9–10% coupon callable in two to three years has roughly 3.5-year duration-to-worst on a six- to seven-year maturity, none of which matures inside this horizon.

#### The real swing factor: graduation

The genuine source of high-yield attrition is not time but **migration**. Morgan Stanley is explicit: "Project-level debt can migrate higher in quality as construction risk burns off… After the non-call period, stabilized assets may also be able to refinance at lower costs in the ABS or IG markets… older data-center vintages can upgrade or migrate out of the asset class, freeing capacity for new project-level issuance" ([p. 10](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Credit_Strategy_A_Break_from_the_Heat_For_Now-2026-08-14.pdf>)).

**No source quantifies this rate.** It is therefore presented as the explicit scenario axis rather than buried inside a blended parameter. Of J.P. Morgan's $350bn cumulative 2026–30 forecast, $34.2bn is already done ($31.1bn of high-yield bonds plus the $3.1bn CoreWeave loan), leaving **$315.8bn**. Of that, ~70% is assumed to price as bonds rather than loans — below J.P. Morgan's 2026 mix of 81% because the same note states "the leveraged loan market is going to fund an increasing part of the AI capex buildout in Leveraged Finance over the next year".

| End-2030 HPC scenario | Graduation | Retained | $N$ | 2030 weight |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Heavy graduation** (low) | Most 2026–27 vintages upgrade or refinance out | 25% | $55bn | **5.6%** |
| **Moderate** (base) | Roughly half migrate | 50% | $111bn | **8.6%** |
| **Low graduation** (high) | Migration matches the observed 2026 rate | 80% | $177bn | **11.9%** |

> **Preferred forward view: HPC high yield rises from 2.75–3.0% today to approximately 8.5% by end-2030, within a 6–12% range, with the graduation rate to investment grade and ABS/CMBS as the dominant uncertainty.**

Morgan Stanley's ~$170bn capacity to a 15% threshold is not binding on any of these cases. J.P. Morgan separately notes that high-yield Technology, at 8.31% of the index, "could easily double in size over the next 5 years (+$100bn)" without reaching post-GFC Energy's peak ([p. 40](<research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf>)) — consistent with the base and high cases.

**Note the sign of the graduation risk.** A low 2030 high-yield weight is not a benign outcome. Every dollar that graduates out of high yield reappears in investment grade, ABS or CMBS. The heavy-graduation case is simultaneously the *highest* case for cross-wrapper migration, and it is the reason concentration must be reported on a look-through basis across all four benchmarks rather than one at a time.

### 3.3 The run-rate tension

The most decision-relevant number in this section is not a scenario but a gap.

AI-related issuers accounted for roughly **one third of 2026 net investment-grade issuance**, which Apollo puts "on pace to hit $1 trillion this year" — implying **~$333bn of AI net IG issuance in 2026 alone** ([Apollo, pp. 12, 18](<research/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf>)). Apollo's own absorption ceiling is under $1,000bn **through 2030**.

Those two figures are compatible only if AI net investment-grade issuance falls to roughly **$165bn a year across 2027–30 — a halving from the current pace.** Both Apollo and J.P. Morgan do forecast hyperscaler borrowing peaking in 2027, so a decline is the consensus view. But if the 2026 run-rate merely persists rather than halving, $N$ is ~$1,350bn and the 2030 weight is **15–16%**, above every published anchor.

The underlying cash-flow driver argues against an early decline. Morgan Stanley Equity Research estimates imply a **57% increase in total capex** for Amazon, Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft going into 2027, with combined free cash flow falling from **about +$20bn in 2026 to −$140bn in 2027** ([Morgan Stanley, pp. 2–3](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Credit_Strategy_A_Break_from_the_Heat_For_Now-2026-08-14.pdf>)). On those estimates the financing gap widens next year rather than narrowing. Issuance can pause after a heavy summer; the 12–18 month funding requirement does not.

A second 2027 convergence compounds this. Apollo identifies **more than $100bn of software and services loans maturing between 2027 and 2029** ([Apollo, p. 11](<research/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf>)) — the disruption sleeve of §1 — landing in the same window as the hyperscaler borrowing peak and the widening free-cash-flow gap. Buildout credit and disruption credit can therefore come under refinancing pressure simultaneously, which is precisely the correlation a sector-diversified book would not expect.

**Monitoring implication:** AI share of net investment-grade issuance is the single highest-value indicator on the dashboard. It is observable quarterly, it discriminates between the base case and the stress case within two to three quarters, and no other series does so as early.

### 3.4 Structured credit

The Structured Finance Association projects data-centre securitizations rising from approximately **$61bn in 2026 to $180bn by end-2028**, reaching about **20% of esoteric ABS and 15% of SASB CMBS**. That is a concentration forecast within two structured-credit subsectors, not within the whole bond market. [Structured Finance Association, July 2026](https://structuredfinance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/SFA-Research-Corner_How-Data-Center-ABS-and-CMBS-Fit-in-a-Broader-Financing-Ecosystem.pdf).

This is the destination for both the investment-grade residual — Apollo's >$1tn that public IG cannot absorb — and the high-yield graduation flow in §3.2. The three should be monitored as one migration, not as three sectors.

## 4. Evidence, assumptions and remaining gaps

The directional conclusion is strong: AI financing is broadening from a small group of cash-rich hyperscalers into data-centre, semiconductor, power and structured-credit borrowers, and current issuance is running well ahead of stock weights. The precise future weights are less certain.

- **Constituent transparency:** the five-name hyperscaler basket is reproducible, but J.P. Morgan does not publish the full narrow, broad or 12-name HPC security lists. The 6.2%, 15.5% and 2.75% figures are reported observations, while their implied market sizes are calculations. The source-linked audit bridge is in [the reconciliation workbook](<ai_reponse/ai-credit-stock-flow-rebuild.xlsx>).
- **Eligibility and denominator:** the $2.1tn and $350bn forecasts span currencies and legal wrappers, and deal-level index inclusion, issue size and rating are not supplied. The forward scenarios therefore avoid applying a single judgement retention factor to a gross global forecast, and instead triangulate three routes whose numerators are already denominated in index units (§3.0). The investment-grade denominator has been moved to **ICE BofA US Corporate (C0A0), $9.4tn dirty market value at August 2026**, which reconciles to J.P. Morgan's implied $9.03tn within 4%; the high-yield denominator remains a back-solve pending H0A0.
- **State the denominator before comparing any two figures.** Every apparent contradiction resolved in this section — 4.15% vs 6.2% vs 15.5%, $9.03tn vs $9.4tn vs $10tn, $679bn vs $898bn vs $1,000bn, 2.75% vs 3% vs 5% in high yield — proved to be a scope or denominator difference rather than a disagreement. Two errors were made during drafting by comparing figures across unstated denominators, in opposite directions. No capacity, stock or flow figure should enter this section without its universe, currency, basis and date recorded alongside it.
- **Two houses converge once put on a common denominator.** Morgan Stanley's $679–711bn capacity figures reproduce on a single implied index base of $7.15tn with a 6.0% start. That base sits below the same note's "approximately US$10 trillion" because the latter describes the IG *market* and the former an *index* — a distinction that must be preserved whenever the two are compared. Rescaled to C0A0, Morgan Stanley implies $898–935bn against Apollo's independently derived sub-$1tn ceiling. Neither house can see this agreement, because neither states its denominator in the other's terms (§3.1).
- **Superseded figures.** Apollo's 6.8%-by-2030 hyperscaler path, cited in earlier drafts, is superseded by the same house's August 2026 update at 8.8%. Any prior conclusion of a "6–8% 2030 core-obligor range" should be withdrawn.
- **Duration is not maturity.** J.P. Morgan's "average duration inside 3.5 years" for HPC high yield is quoted alongside spread-to-worst and is measured to call. It does not indicate that HPC bonds leave the index within 3.5 years, and it should not be used as an amortisation input (§3.2).
- **Broad-ecosystem attribution:** 15.5% treats whole utility and industrial issuers as part of the ecosystem. Without contract-level load, customer or backlog shares, it is an upper exposure envelope. The narrow 6.2% measure should remain the headline stock estimate.
- **Hidden and shifting exposure:** SPVs, private placements, municipal prepays, leases and purchase commitments can be off-index, while later securitization can make one project appear in several issuance datasets. The five hyperscalers disclose more than $660bn of leases and more than $640bn of purchase commitments, but these categories overlap and have different timing; they cannot simply be added to debt. Missing exposure is unknown, not zero, and cumulative issuance across wrappers must be deduplicated. [Morgan Stanley, *Global Fixed Income Outlook*, pp. 12–18](<research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf>).
- **Forecast scope has changed:** Morgan Stanley's older $2.9tn data-centre-spending estimate covered 2025–28 and excluded power, while J.P. Morgan's $5.5tn runs through 2030 and captures a broader, later buildout. The difference is mostly horizon and scope, not a like-for-like revision. Morgan Stanley itself described the earlier $1.5tn financing-gap allocation as assumption-heavy. [Morgan Stanley, *Bridging a $1.5tn Data Center Financing Gap*, pp. 1–16](<research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Tech_Diffusion_and_GenAI_Bridging_a_1_5tr_Data_Center_Financing_Gap-2025-07-16.pdf>).
- **Credit risk is not market-value weight:** hyperscaler IG debt may have low default risk even as its index weight rises; highly levered HPC and software borrowers have much larger refinancing and loss sensitivity. Concentration reporting should therefore pair weights with spread duration, rating, maturity, collateral and tenant/offtaker concentration.
- **Disruption is a separate tail:** sector weights of 3.5% in HY and 16% in loans are useful screening envelopes, but they neither establish that every software borrower is impaired nor measure loss severity. Morgan Stanley notes that more than 80% of software loan issuers are private/sponsor-backed and that definitions vary across data providers. [Morgan Stanley, *Global Fixed Income Outlook*, pp. 12–22](<research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf>); [Morgan Stanley, *Mapping Software Exposure in Leveraged Credit*, pp. 1–15](<research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Credit_and_CLO_Strategy_Mapping_Software_Exposure_in_Leveraged_Credit-2026-02-09.pdf>).

The practical conclusion is to monitor **6.2% narrow IG and 2.75–3.0% HPC HY as current stock**, **15.5% broad IG as the common-factor envelope**, and stock/flow gaps and wrapper migration as the leading indicators. The end-2030 planning ranges are **9.5–13% narrow IG with an 11% base**, **more than 20% broad IG**, and **6–12% HPC HY with an 8.5% base**, on the sourced routes set out in §3.

Three caveats govern their use. First, the high-yield range is driven almost entirely by a graduation rate that **no source quantifies**; a low 2030 high-yield weight is not benign, because it means the exposure has migrated to investment grade, ABS or CMBS rather than disappeared. Second, the ranges assume non-AI index growth of 3–5% and are sensitive to it by roughly 1pp; this input is a placeholder pending the C0A0 and H0A0 series (§3.5). Third, the **15–16% run-rate stress case (§3.3) is not a tail scenario** — it is what happens if 2026 issuance behaviour simply continues, and it should be carried alongside the base case rather than below it.

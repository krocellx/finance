# Existing Research Findings on Cross-Asset AI Concentration

**Date reviewed:** 17 August 2026  
**Research question:** How concentrated is AI-related economic and investment exposure across public equities, corporate credit, and private markets today, and how could it evolve over the next three to five years?

## Executive conclusion

The reports provide enough evidence to answer the research question directionally, but not enough to calculate one defensible, portfolio-wide AI concentration percentage.

Collectively, the research concludes that:

- Today's direct exposure remains highly concentrated in a small group of US hyperscalers, semiconductor companies, and Asian technology exporters.
- Economic exposure is broadening into data centres, power, utilities, industrial equipment, private credit, and corporate adopters.
- That broadening does not necessarily create real diversification. Many apparently separate positions ultimately depend on the same hyperscaler capital-expenditure budgets, AI demand assumptions, financing conditions, and power availability.
- Credit concentration is currently more visible in new issuance and contractual commitments than in outstanding benchmark weights.
- Private-market estimates are predominantly forward financing forecasts rather than observed portfolio exposures.
- No report calculates a consistent AI-weighted concentration measure across equities, credit, and private assets. Constructing that measure remains the project's principal original analytical contribution.

Page references below refer to PDF-viewer pages.

## Most useful existing findings

### 1. US equity concentration is already near historic highs

**Evidence status:** Observed result

Technology and Communication Services generated 54% of US earnings growth in 1Q26. J.P. Morgan also finds S&P 500 constituent-weight concentration, measured by the Gini coefficient, near its historical high.

Four hyperscalers guided to approximately $695–725 billion of 2026 capital expenditure.

**Report conclusion:** Current equity concentration is reinforced by both market-cap leadership and AI-related earnings and capital spending.

**Important limitation:** The Gini measures total S&P 500 concentration, not specifically AI concentration.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *All the Rest Is No-AI*, PDF-viewer pp. 5–8.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-The_JP_Morgan_View_All_the_Rest_is_No_AI-2026-05-08.pdf)

### 2. Japan provides the clearest benchmark-level AI concentration result

**Evidence status:** Observed and analyst-mapped result

A 50-company AI, semiconductor, and data-centre cohort represents:

- 33.7% of TOPIX 500.
- 54.8% of Nikkei 225.

The same cohort is expected to generate approximately 59% FY26 profit growth. J.P. Morgan separately estimates AI-semiconductor exposure at 20–30% of TOPIX and just under 50% of Nikkei 225.

During the correction examined in the report, KOSPI and the Nikkei Semiconductor Index fell 27% and 30%, respectively, versus 11% for Nikkei 225.

**Report conclusion:** National benchmarks can effectively become concentrated expressions of US hyperscaler capital expenditure, even when the underlying companies are geographically diversified.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *Nikkei 225/KOSPI Correlation and Outlook*, PDF-viewer pp. 1–4.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-Japan_Equity_Strategy_Nikkei_225_KOSPI_Correlation_and_Outlook-2026-07-27.pdf)

### 3. AI economic exposure is broadening well beyond technology

**Evidence status:** Analyst mapping

Morgan Stanley's approximately 3,600-stock survey found:

- 412 companies representing $8.7 trillion received AI-exposure upgrades.
- 259 companies representing $8.5 trillion received materiality upgrades.
- 102 companies representing $2.4 trillion received both.

Its subsequent mapping finds positive AI roles across approximately 78% of Information Technology coverage, 88% of Utilities, 64% of Industrials, and 84% of Financials.

**Report conclusion:** AI exposure is spreading from enablers to adopters, utilities, financials, industrials, and real estate.

**Important limitation:** Morgan Stanley explicitly says these classifications are qualitative. They do not estimate the percentage of each company's revenue, profit, or capital expenditure attributable to AI.

**References:**

- Morgan Stanley, *Mapping AI's Rate of Change: The Broadening Trend Continues*, PDF-viewer pp. 9–21. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Mapping_AIs_Rate_of_Change_The_Broadening_Trend_Continues-2025-07-20.pdf)
- Morgan Stanley, *Mapping AI's Rate of Change: Where the Rubber Meets the Road*, PDF-viewer pp. 4–14 and 50–51. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Mapping_AIs_Rate_of_Change_Where_the_Rubber_Meets_the_Road-2026-02-11.pdf)

### 4. Hyperscalers still control the investment cycle

**Evidence status:** Forecast result

J.P. Morgan projects AI capital expenditure of approximately $900 billion in 2026 and more than $1.2 trillion in 2027. Hyperscalers account for approximately 87% in both years.

AI-related companies are expected to generate 59% of projected 2026 S&P 500 capital expenditure.

The four hyperscalers reported $599 billion of trailing net income but only $169 billion of free cash flow, a $430 billion gap.

**Report conclusion:** Demand may be broadening, but the spending decision remains concentrated in a handful of companies. The free-cash-flow gap increases dependence on debt, leases, special-purpose vehicles, and other financing structures.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *US Equity Strategy: Market Update—Earnings, AI Capex and Monetization*, PDF-viewer pp. 1–2.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-US_Equity_Strategy_Market_Update_Earnings_AI_Capex_and_Monetization-2026-08-10.pdf)

### 5. Credit exposure is much more concentrated in new issuance than in benchmark stock

**Evidence status:** Observed result

J.P. Morgan estimates:

- Direct hyperscaler, data-centre, and semiconductor exposure is 6.2% of high-grade credit, up from 3.9% at the end of 2024.
- Its broader AI ecosystem definition reaches 15.5%, or approximately $1.4 trillion.
- High-yield HPC/data-centre debt represents only 2.75% of outstanding high yield but generated 41% of non-refinancing high-yield issuance year to date.

**Report conclusion:** Existing bond indices may still appear moderately exposed, while marginal credit formation is already heavily AI-driven. Flow measures give a much earlier warning than outstanding index weights.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *AI Capex 2.0: If You Build It, They Will Finance It*, PDF-viewer pp. 32 and 36–37.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf)

### 6. A large part of AI credit risk sits outside conventional bond benchmarks

**Evidence status:** Observed result

Morgan Stanley identifies $336 billion of AI/data-centre debt issuance through 10 July 2026, versus $217 billion during all of 2025.

It also identifies:

- Approximately $53 billion of construction bonds that are not index eligible.
- Approximately $45 billion of data-centre ABS and more than $20 billion of CMBS.
- Rising use of special-purpose vehicles, leases, purchase commitments, guarantees, and backstops.
- Loosening high-yield structures, including higher loan-to-cost ratios, slower amortisation, and weaker tenant-protection terms.

**Report conclusion:** Conventional corporate-bond benchmarks understate total AI financing exposure. Holdings must be mapped across corporate bonds, ABS, CMBS, construction loans, project finance, and special-purpose vehicles without double-counting assets that migrate between wrappers.

**Reference:** Morgan Stanley, *US Corporate and Securitized Credit: Data Center/AI Financing Investor Feedback*, PDF-viewer pp. 2–16.  
[Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Corporate_and_Securitized_Credit_Data_Center_AI_Financing_Investor_Feedback-2026-07-13.pdf)

### 7. Contractual obligations are becoming as important as reported debt

**Evidence status:** Observed disclosures

Morgan Stanley reports more than:

- $660 billion of lease commitments across five hyperscalers.
- $640 billion of disclosed purchase commitments linked to data centres.

These categories should not automatically be added together because their definitions, timing, and potential overlap differ.

**Report conclusion:** Debt alone is an incomplete measure. A proper concentration analysis must include leases, take-or-pay agreements, purchase obligations, guarantees, and backstops.

**Reference:** Morgan Stanley, *Global Fixed Income Outlook: April 2026*, PDF-viewer pp. 16–17.  
[Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf)

### 8. Private credit has both positive and negative AI exposure

**Evidence status:** Observed portfolio exposure

Software represents approximately:

- 26% of public BDC portfolios.
- 19% of private-credit CLOs.
- 16% of US leveraged loans.
- 15% of broadly syndicated CLOs.
- 10% of European loans and CLOs.
- 3–3.5% of high-yield bonds.

Some individual BDCs have software exposure above 50%, and some private-credit CLO managers exceed 30%.

**Report conclusion:** Private credit is exposed on two sides:

1. Positively through data-centre and power financing.
2. Negatively through leveraged software companies that could be disrupted by AI.

The percentages come from different universes and should not be aggregated into one total.

**References:**

- Morgan Stanley, *Mapping Software Exposure in Leveraged Credit*, PDF-viewer pp. 4–18. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Credit_and_CLO_Strategy_Mapping_Software_Exposure_in_Leveraged_Credit-2026-02-09.pdf)
- Morgan Stanley, *Global Fixed Income Outlook: April 2026*, PDF-viewer pp. 19–22. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf)

### 9. The projected private-financing requirement is enormous but highly assumption-dependent

**Evidence status:** Forecast results

- Morgan Stanley estimates $2.9 trillion of data-centre spending through 2028 and derives an approximately $800 billion private-credit requirement as a residual financing need.
- J.P. Morgan forecasts $5.5 trillion of AI capital expenditure through 2030, including $4.1 trillion of debt and an approximately $1.4 trillion call on private and alternative capital.

**Report conclusion:** AI exposure is likely to migrate substantially into private markets over the next three to five years.

**Critical qualification:** These are scenario-based financing forecasts with different scopes and horizons. They are not current private-market exposure figures and should not be averaged.

**References:**

- Morgan Stanley, *Bridging a $1.5tn Data Center Financing Gap*, PDF-viewer pp. 6–34. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Tech_Diffusion_and_GenAI_Bridging_a_1_5tr_Data_Center_Financing_Gap-2025-07-16.pdf)
- J.P. Morgan, *AI Capex 2.0*, PDF-viewer pp. 1–3 and 47–48. [Open report](research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf)

### 10. Power is emerging as the binding physical constraint

**Evidence status:** Forecast and pipeline evidence

J.P. Morgan cites 2030 US data-centre demand forecasts of:

- 118 GW in the base case.
- 160 GW in an AI-chip-driven case.

Announced pipelines materially overstate executable capacity. In ERCOT, approximately 434 GW had been proposed, versus 134 GW contracted, 39 GW cleared, and only 11 GW under construction.

Lead times reach 36–60 months for heavy equipment and 48–84 months for grid interconnection.

**Report conclusion:** Headline data-centre pipelines require probability and development-stage haircuts. Power access, transformers, interconnection, and skilled labour may become more concentrated than the data-centre operators themselves.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *Hardware and Networking: Key Takeaways on Data Center Demand*, PDF-viewer pp. 1–2.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-Hardware_and_Networking_Key_Takeaways_on_Data_Center_Demand-2026-07-27.pdf)

### 11. Apparent asset diversification can conceal the same underlying tenant

J.P. Morgan recommends measuring the spread of each financing instrument relative to its ultimate hyperscaler exposure.

The same data-centre campus can appear separately as:

- A hyperscaler corporate bond.
- A developer's high-yield bond.
- A private construction loan.
- ABS or CMBS.
- Infrastructure-equity ownership.
- A utility or power-generation investment.
- A hyperscaler lease or purchase commitment.

**Report conclusion:** Asset-class labels are insufficient. Exposure should be consolidated by ultimate tenant, campus, power market, and capital-expenditure programme.

**Reference:** J.P. Morgan, *AI Capex 2.0*, PDF-viewer p. 49.  
[Open report](research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf)

### 12. Conventional risk models can misclassify AI dependence as idiosyncratic risk

**Evidence status:** Model result

Morgan Stanley finds that sector, country, and style models can leave thematic AI co-movement in residual or “specific” risk.

Adding thematic factors explains 50–100% more systematic risk for a concentrated top-performing US portfolio during stress periods.

**Report conclusion:** Two companies in different sectors can still be exposed to the same AI factor. Sector diversification alone may materially overstate portfolio diversification.

**Important limitation:** The effect is strongest in concentrated thematic portfolios and stress periods; it is smaller and less stable in broad benchmarks and some non-US markets.

**References:**

- Morgan Stanley, *Beyond Traditional Factors: Enhancing Portfolio Risk Attribution*, PDF-viewer pp. 2–17. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Quant_Matters_Beyond_Traditional_Factors_Enhancing_Portfolio_Risk_Attribution-2025-10-20.pdf)
- Morgan Stanley, *Mapping the Thematic Landscape and Risk Attribution*. [Open report](research/Morgan_Stanley-Quant_Matters_Mapping_the_Thematic_Landscape_and_Risk_Attribution-2026-02-20.pdf)

### 13. The reports distinguish systemic AI shocks from value-redistribution shocks

Goldman Sachs divides AI risks into:

- **Aggregate-value shocks:** Lower adoption, weaker productivity, financing constraints, or higher discount rates. These affect broad equities, credit, and rates.
- **Distributional shocks:** Changes in which hyperscaler, supplier, country, or adopter captures the value. These mainly cause rotations between AI-related positions.

In selected aggregate-shock episodes, Goldman's AI basket fell 6–9.5% and the S&P 500 fell 1.5–2.6%. Distributional announcements caused large supplier/hyperscaler rotations but little S&P 500 or Treasury movement.

**Report conclusion:** Not every AI sell-off is systemic. The concentration framework should model aggregate and distributional scenarios separately.

**Reference:** Goldman Sachs, *Distinguishing AI Risks: Size of the Pie vs Share of the Pie*, PDF-viewer pp. 4–11.  
[Open report](research/Goldman_Sachs-Global_Markets_Comment_Distinguishing_AI_Risks_Size_of_the_Pie_vs_Share_of_the_Pie-2026-07-23.pdf)

### 14. Fundamentals are real, but market values may assume unusually persistent profits

Goldman Sachs estimates that AI-related companies have gained approximately $27 trillion of market value since November 2022. Its conservative AI attribution is $14–17 trillion, compared with a baseline present value of approximately $9 trillion for additional US AI capital revenues.

**Report conclusion:** This is not necessarily a 1999-style balance-sheet or valuation bubble. However, markets may be extrapolating temporary capital-expenditure-boom earnings several years beyond their sustainable duration—an “earnings bubble” risk.

**Reference:** Goldman Sachs, *Where the AI Boom Stands Now: Markets Ahead of the Macro*, PDF-viewer pp. 2–11.  
[Open report](research/Goldman_Sachs-Global_Markets_Analyst_Where_the_AI_Boom_Stands_Now_Markets_Ahead_of_the_Macro-2026-06-22.pdf)

## What the reports do not answer

The research packet does not yet provide:

- A reproducible AI-weighted S&P 500 or MSCI World concentration measure.
- A security-level mapping across equities, corporate bonds, ABS, CMBS, and private holdings.
- A total-portfolio AI exposure percentage for a pension fund.
- A private-market benchmark with complete asset or ultimate-tenant holdings.
- Consistent definitions and denominators across reports.
- A demonstrated causal link between every mapped adopter and AI-driven earnings.

The 17–32% total-fund exposure and 64% stress-loss concentration appearing in some existing AI-generated responses are model illustrations, not conclusions directly reported by the sell-side PDFs. They should not be cited as established findings.

## Best synthesis

> AI exposure is currently concentrated where investment decisions and profits originate—hyperscalers, semiconductors, and selected Asian benchmarks. Over the next three to five years, exposure is likely to spread into credit, private capital, power, utilities, industrial equipment, and corporate adopters. However, this represents broader ownership of the same AI investment cycle more than independent diversification, because much of the ecosystem remains dependent on a small number of hyperscaler tenants, capital-expenditure budgets, and physical bottlenecks.

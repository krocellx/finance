# Private Credit — AI concentration

*Evidence cut-off: 18 August 2026. Stock observations are end-2025 or 1H26 unless stated. Forward horizon is five years, end-2025 to end-2030.*

## Conclusion

Private credit carries **three different AI concentrations that must be reported separately and never added**. The narrow, positive, *measured* exposure is **~8% of outstanding direct loans (>$200bn)**. A much larger **AI-infrastructure sleeve — $800bn to $1.4tn of forecast financing — is almost entirely invisible to that measure**, because it sits in asset-based finance, private investment-grade and insurance structures that the standard taxonomies exclude on both sides of the ratio. Separately, a **19–26% software/technology book** is exposed to AI with the opposite sign.

> **Preferred forward view: the narrow AI cohort moves from 8% today to approximately 10% of outstanding direct loans by end-2030, within a 5–14% range.** The base case is not a large re-rating, because the asset class itself is forecast to grow 11–14% a year. The 5% floor is the case in which AI origination volumes simply hold at the 2025 pace — and it is a *falling* weight, not a rising one.

The single most important structural finding is that **the 2030 weight converges toward the AI share of new originations, not toward any capex forecast**. With a 4.7-year average loan life on a book compounding at 11%, five years covers ~82% of that convergence. Every published headline about trillions of AI financing is a statement about *flow*; the portfolio weight is a lagged transformation of it, and the lag is the whole story.

| Measure | Today | 2030 | Status | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| **Narrow** — AI-native borrowers, direct loans | **~8%; >$200bn** | **~10%; 5–14%** | Observed → calculated | The only sleeve with a matched numerator and denominator |
| **Broad** — incl. AI-infrastructure ABF/private IG | **not measured** | **$0.8–1.4tn** | Forecast dollars only | No published denominator exists. Do not express as a % |
| **Disruption** — software / technology screen | **19–26%** | not forecast | Observed | Opposite sign. Not additive to the above |

Three cautions carry to the IC. First, the narrow numerator rests entirely on BIS notes that are **not held in this workspace** and could not be verified line-by-line (see §4). Second, the broad sleeve's absence from the measured number is not conservatism — it is a coverage hole precisely where the growth is. Third, a *low* 2030 narrow weight is not a benign outcome: it most likely means the exposure migrated into ABF, ABS/CMBS and private IG, where this framework currently cannot see it.

## 1. What concentration means and where it stands today

### 1.1 Three cohorts, not one number

"AI concentration" in private credit resolves into three distinct measures. They use different universes, different taxonomies and, critically, **different signs**.

1. **Buildout / narrow.** Loans to borrowers classified as AI-native. This is what the BIS measures and what the market quotes.
2. **Buildout / broad.** Loans whose *use of proceeds, collateral, tenant or offtaker* is AI infrastructure — data centres, GPUs, power, project and construction finance — regardless of how the borrower is classified. This is where every sell-side forecast points and where almost no measurement exists.
3. **Disruption.** Loans to incumbents whose revenue could be impaired by AI substitution. Software is the standard screen.

Cohorts 1 and 2 are **nested**: 1 is a subset of 2. Cohort 3 **overlaps both** and carries the opposite sign. Reporting a single blended "AI exposure" for private credit destroys all three signals.

### 1.2 The narrow cohort — the only matched measurement

Outstanding private-credit loans to AI-related companies rose from near zero to **more than $200bn, almost 8% of outstanding direct-loan volume**. Originations reached **more than $40bn in 2025**, from about $3bn in 2010. Only about **20% of funds** had made an AI-related loan and the average lending fund's exposure was about **5%** — the aggregate is concentrated in larger, more active managers. Loan structures showed no AI premium: **46% secured versus 48%**, spreads of **6.2pp versus 6.1pp**, and average maturity of **4.7 years versus 4.8**, on a much larger average ticket (**$169m versus $90m**). The BIS calls the gap between this pricing and equity-market AI valuations a "schism". ([BIS Bulletin 120, January 2026](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf))

The definition is a **borrower-vertical screen** — PitchBook's *Artificial Intelligence*, *Big Data* and *Cloud Tech* verticals. That cuts two ways at once. It is **too wide** in that Big Data and Cloud Tech capture businesses with no AI-capex linkage. It is **far too narrow** in that a data-centre SPV, a GPU-backed facility, a power project serving a hyperscaler campus, or a private IG placement for a lease-backed compute portfolio will generally not be classified as an "AI company" at all.

**This 8% is the correct current headline, and it is also the reason the headline is misleading.** Every one of the structures listed above is what Morgan Stanley, Apollo and J.P. Morgan independently forecast to be the dominant channel (§1.3).

Five measures from four houses across three universes span **19–26%, mean 21.6%**. The dispersion is taxonomy width and vehicle sample, **not disagreement**. Concentration *within* the screen is the sharper risk. The top five BDCs held **37%** of software lending, the top ten **more than half**, and about **60%** of software lending went to borrowers financed by seven or more BDCs ([BIS Bulletin 128](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull128.htm)). Manager-level diversification is substantially illusory. The IMF notes software exposure can exceed **50% of NAV** in leveraged vehicles even at ~⅕ of direct loans ([IMF *GFSR*, April 2026, ch. 1, p. 20](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).

This screen is **already live, not prospective**. Software equities fell ~30% between October 2025 and February 2026, BDC share prices ~10%, and high-software BDCs underperformed low-software peers by ~5pp ([BIS Quarterly Review, March 2026](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)). Preqin attributes one of the weakest first quarters of private-credit fundraising in years partly to "the impact of AI on software businesses" ([Preqin, *Private Credit Q2 2026*, p. 2](<research/Preqin-Private_Credit_Q2_2026_Quarterly_Update-2026-07-30.pdf>)).

**Adding 8% and 19–26% to report "27–34% AI exposure" would be wrong.** Different universes, different taxonomies, opposite signs, and an unmeasured overlap.

## 2. Recommended measurement framework

### Stock

$$
C^{+}=\frac{\sum_i EAD_i\, b_i}{\sum_i EAD_i},\qquad
C^{-}=\frac{\sum_i EAD_i\, d_i}{\sum_i EAD_i}
$$

`EAD` is funded principal plus a modelled expected draw on unfunded commitments. `b` is the fraction of exposure attributable to AI buildout; `d` the fraction economically vulnerable to AI disruption. Both run 0–1 and must be evidenced by use of proceeds, borrower revenue, collateral, tenant/guarantor, power availability and refinancing dependence. **Unknown exposure stays "unclassified" and is reported as its own line — never defaulted to zero.**

The primary denominator is **gross credit EAD**, because NAV is distorted by vehicle leverage — a distinction that is not academic here, since gross assets are roughly **2.0×** net across the BDC universe ($500bn GAV against $250bn NAV; [Preqin, *BDCs Examined*, p. 2](<research/Preqin-Private_Markets_Unpacked_BDCs_Examined-2026-07-08.pdf>)). Report the same exposures as a percentage of NAV as a secondary loss-amplification view.

### Flow

Track originations separately, and roll them into stock explicitly:

$$
S_{t+1}=S_t+\text{originations}-\text{repayments}-\text{defaults}-\text{sales/securitisations}
$$

§3.3 shows why this roll-forward, and not any capex forecast, determines the answer.

### Look-through and concentration inside the sleeve

Report top-five/top-ten shares and `HHI = Σs²` / `effective N = 1/HHI` by **borrower, ultimate offtaker/tenant, sponsor, manager, campus and power market**. A diversified list of data-centre SPVs can depend on one tenant and one capex budget. Apollo documents at least **$90bn of contractual backstops** from IG technology names supporting "tens of billions" of associated debt across neocloud, chip-leasing and power-as-a-service structures ([Apollo, Exhibit 26, p. 16](<research/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf>)) — the borrower list and the risk list are different lists.

### Four controls

- **Separate stock from flow**, and report both.
- **Preserve the two signs.** Never net or add buildout and disruption.
- **Consolidate to the ultimate obligor**, across every vehicle in the house.
- **Report the unclassified residual.** In private credit it is currently the largest single line (§3.5).

## 3. Forward concentration

### 3.0 Method: one identity, and why private credit needs a second

The forward numbers use the same identity as the public fixed income section:

$$
W_{2030}=\frac{S_{0}+N}{\left(D_{0}-S_{0}\right)\left(1+g\right)^{h}+S_{0}+N}
$$

$D_0$ is the current matched denominator, $S_0=W_0 D_0$ the current AI stock, $N$ the **net new AI loan stock retained by end-2030**, $g$ the growth of the **non-AI** remainder, $h = 5.0$ years from end-2025. As in public credit, $N$ enters numerator *and* denominator, and $g$ applies to the remainder only.

**Private credit needs a second construction that public credit did not.** In investment grade, the 17-year average maturity of hyperscaler paper meant essentially no attrition over the horizon, so cumulative issuance converted almost one-for-one into stock. Here, the average AI loan matures in **4.7 years** ([BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf)) — shorter than the forecast horizon. Roughly **21% of the stock rolls off every year**. Cumulative-financing forecasts therefore cannot be added to today's stock, and the identity alone cannot answer the question. §3.3 adds the flow recursion that can.

**What the prior version of this section did.** It divided the BIS's $300–600bn numerator by Preqin's $4.5tn total private-credit AUM forecast. That is a **basis mismatch in both directions**: the numerator is a gross, levered, direct-loan stock on a borrower-vertical taxonomy, while the denominator is an AUM series that includes dry powder, includes mezzanine, distressed, special-situations and venture debt — and, decisively, **explicitly excludes the structures where the AI money is going** (§3.1). The two errors partly offset, which is why the published 7–13% survives roughly intact. It was not, however, supported as constructed.

### 3.1 The denominator

Preqin states its own exclusion plainly. The $4.5tn forecast is built from a closed-end fund model plus a top-down BDC estimate, and:

> "The model also does not incorporate structures outside the traditional closed-end fund, such as **investment grade private credit, investments by insurance companies via separately managed accounts, and private asset-based finance (ABF)**." — [Preqin, *Private Markets in 2030*, p. 17](<research/Preqin-Private_Markets_in_2030-2025-10-10.pdf>)

Those three exclusions are, precisely and exhaustively, the channels Morgan Stanley ("led by ABF"), Apollo ("private IG") and J.P. Morgan ("Apollo and KKR through their insurance affiliates") name as the AI-financing route. **A numerator built on AI infrastructure cannot be divided by this denominator.**

The matched denominator is instead the one implied by the BIS's own two figures, cross-checked bottom-up:

| Route | $bn | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| **BIS implied** — $200bn ÷ 8% | **2,500** | [BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf) |
| Drawdown funds, invested | 1,270 | [J.P. Morgan, p. 41](<research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf>), citing Preqin at 30-Sep ($1.81tn AUM = $543bn dry powder + $1.27tn invested) |
| BDC gross assets | 500 | [Preqin, *BDCs Examined*, p. 2](<research/Preqin-Private_Markets_Unpacked_BDCs_Examined-2026-07-08.pdf>), YE2025 |
| **Subtotal before drawdown-fund leverage** | **1,770** | |
| Residual vs BIS implied | 730 | implies **0.57× leverage** on invested drawdown capital |

A 0.57× leverage ratio on direct-lending funds is unremarkable — the typical range is 0.5–1.0×, and the BDC universe itself runs at **1.0× debt-to-equity** (2.0× gross-to-net). The Federal Reserve independently reports **$425bn gross against $241bn net** across perpetual BDCs and interval funds, a 1.76× gross-to-net on a different sample ([FSR, May 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20260508.pdf)). Two regulators and one data vendor therefore put semi-liquid private-credit gearing in the same place. The reconciliation holds without strain — but note that the residual is an **inference, not a published figure**, and it is the one soft joint in the denominator.

A third check: Preqin's $2.1tn total AUM for 2024, grown at its own implied 13.5% CAGR, gives **$2.38tn at end-2025**, consistent with the BIS's separately stated "private credit AUM of more than $2.2tn".

**$D_0 = \$2,500bn$ is used**, on a gross outstanding direct-loan basis, matching the BIS numerator exactly.

**Growth of the remainder.** Three published rates bracket $g$:

| $g$ | Basis | Source |
|---:|---|---|
| **11.2%** | Direct lending AUM CAGR, 2024–2030F | [Preqin, *Private Markets in 2030*, p. 19](<research/Preqin-Private_Markets_in_2030-2025-10-10.pdf>) — "approaching $1.7tn by 2030F" |
| **13.5%** | Total private credit AUM CAGR, implied by $2.1tn → $4.5tn | Same, p. 17 |
| **14.0%** | Private credit universe CAGR since 2020 | [J.P. Morgan, p. 41](<research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf>) |

All three are **total** growth rates that already include AI lending. Using them as the *non-AI* rate $g$ therefore grows the denominator slightly too fast and **biases every result downward**. The bias is small at an 8% starting weight, and the direction is stated so the IC can read the numbers as conservative.

The base is Route A-mid across the $g$ band (9.4–10.3%), corroborated by the independent Route D recursion at the origination share BIS's own midpoint implies (10.5%, §3.3). The ceiling rises to 14% because Route D reaches 14.1% at BIS's high case. The **floor is materially lower than the prior version's 6.7%**, because that version had no case in which the weight *falls* — and flat originations produce exactly that.

### 3.3 The origination-share result

This is the structural finding of the section.

Model the AI sleeve as a roll-forward with average loan life $M = 4.7$ years and an AI share $\sigma$ of new originations. A book of size $D$ growing at $g$ with run-off $1/M$ requires **gross originations of $D(g + 1/M)$** — enough to replace maturities *and* fund growth:

$$
S_{t+1}=S_t\left(1-\tfrac{1}{M}\right)+\sigma\,D_t\left(g+\tfrac{1}{M}\right)
$$

Setting $s = S/D$ constant gives $s(g+1/M) = \sigma(g+1/M)$, so **$s \to \sigma$ exactly**. The error decays by $(1-1/M)/(1+g)$ a year, so at $g = 11.2\%$ five years covers **82%** of the distance from today's weight to that share.

> **The 2030 portfolio weight converges toward the AI share of new lending, and toward nothing else.** No capex forecast enters. Every trillion-dollar headline in §1.3 is a statement about flow; the weight is a lagged transformation of it.

| AI share of new originations | Implied 2030 weight | What it corresponds to |
|---:|---:|---|
| 4.9% | 5.5% | **Flat originations at the observed 2025 pace** |
| 6.9% | 7.1% | BIS's own $300bn low case |
| 9.0% | 8.8% | OECD 2024 deal share |
| **11.1%** | **10.5%** | **BIS's own $450bn midpoint** |
| 15.4% | 14.1% | BIS's own $600bn high case |
| 17.0% | 15.4% | FSB five-year average deal share |
| 34.0% | 29.4% | OECD **and** FSB 2025 deal share (they agree) |

Two independent readings of this table matter.

**First, it corroborates Route A without sharing its construction.** The identity in §3.2 grows a non-AI base at $g$ and adds $N$ on top; this recursion grows the *whole* book at $g$ and lets AI take a share of the flow. At BIS's own targets the two agree to within 0.8pp (7.12% vs 7.06%, 10.32% vs 10.55%, 13.30% vs 14.08%). That is a genuine robustness result, not a restatement.

**Second, it prices BIS's projection against observed deal flow.** BIS's $300–600bn implies an AI origination share of **7–15%**. The FSB's five-year average deal share is 17% and the 2025 reading — on which the OECD and FSB independently agree — was 34%. **On its own arithmetic, the BIS projection is conservative** relative to the flow evidence — the opposite of how it is usually read.

*(Deal-share figures: [OECD, Global Debt Report 2026, ch. 2](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/global-debt-report-2026_e9d80efd-en/full-report/corporate-debt-market-outlook-in-a-transforming-world_cf86a220.html) for 9% in 2024 and 34% in 2025; [FSB, Vulnerabilities in Private Credit, May 2026](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P060526.pdf) for 34% in 2025 against a 17% five-year average. OECD deal value rose $97bn → $174bn, so 34% implies ~$59bn of AI deal value — directionally consistent with the BIS's ">$40bn" of originations on a broader universe.)*

**Deal-share and origination-share are not the same statistic and must not be substituted for one another.** The OECD measures recorded *deal value* in a coverage-limited sample dominated by large transactions; the BIS measures *direct-loan originations* across a much broader base. The table uses the OECD series as a plausible range for $\sigma$, not as a measurement of it. Reading "5–34%" as a range for current concentration would be wrong.

### 3.4 The run-rate tension

The most decision-relevant number here is a gap, not a scenario — and it is the mirror image of the one in public fixed income.

Two BIS figures, taken together, pin the current run-rate exactly:

- AI-related originations were **more than $40bn in 2025**
- The average AI loan matures in **4.7 years**

A stock $S$ with average life $M$ requires originations of $S/M$ each year simply to stand still. For the observed $200bn stock that is **$200bn ÷ 4.7 = $42.6bn a year** — which is, to within rounding, exactly what was originated.

**The 2025 origination pace is the replacement rate.** It grows the AI stock by approximately nothing. Against an asset class compounding at 11–14%, that is a weight falling from 8% to ~4.4–4.9%, not a rising one.

BIS's own 2030 projection therefore carries an implicit and unstated flow requirement:

| To reach a 2030 stock of | Sustained originations needed | Multiple of the 2025 pace |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $300bn | $63.8bn/yr | **1.6×** |
| $450bn | $95.7bn/yr | **2.4×** |
| $600bn | $127.7bn/yr | **3.2×** |

The direction is plausible — the OECD's jump from 9% to 34% of deal value points the same way, and the AI-infrastructure pipeline in §1.3 is enormous. But it is a **requirement, not an observation**, and no source in this set states it.

**Monitoring implication: AI share of private-credit originations is the single highest-value indicator on the dashboard.** It is the variable the 2030 weight converges to, it discriminates between a 4% and a 13% outcome, and it is observable from deal tapes and BDC filings well before it shows up in stock.

### 3.5 The broad sleeve — dollars, and an honest blank

The AI-infrastructure cohort cannot responsibly be given a percentage, because **no published denominator covers it**. Preqin excludes ABF, private IG and insurance SMAs by its own statement (§3.1); the BIS direct-loan universe excludes them by taxonomy. Morgan Stanley states only that the ABF total addressable market is "significantly larger than direct lending" without a figure in the text.

What can be stated:

| Numerator | $bn | Window | Basis |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Morgan Stanley — private credit for data centres | **~800** | 2025–28 | cumulative gross, led by ABF |
| Apollo — private IG financing of the AI ecosystem | **>1,000** | to 2030 | cumulative gross |
| J.P. Morgan — private credit / alternative capital | **up to 1,400** | to 2030 | includes infrastructure **equity** |

To convert any of these into a weight, the denominator must be supplied. The arithmetic is linear and the workbook holds it live:

| If the broad private-credit universe in 2030 is | $800bn implies | $1,000bn implies |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $6tn | 13.3% | 16.7% |
| $8tn | 10.0% | 12.5% |
| $10tn | 8.0% | 10.0% |

These are **illustrations of an identity, not estimates**, and are labelled as such in the workbook. They are shown because the IC will otherwise mentally divide $800bn by $4.5tn and reach ~18% — which is the specific error this section exists to prevent.

Note also that these are *cumulative gross* financing over three-to-five years against a book that turns over every 4.7 years, so they are **not** standing stock. Running Morgan Stanley's $800bn (≈$200bn a year, 2025–28) through the §3.3 roll-forward:

| End-2030 standing stock from the $800bn | $bn |
|---|---:|
| Peak, end-2028 | **579** |
| If originations stop after 2028 (the MS window read literally) | **359** |
| If the $200bn/yr pace continues through 2030 | **716** |

The stock peaks *below* the gross figure in every case and can be less than half of it. This is the arithmetic that "add $800bn to today's $200bn" gets wrong, and it is before any take-out into ABS/CMBS (§3.6).

### 3.6 Where the exposure goes when it leaves

Private credit's analogue of high yield's "graduation" problem is the **ABS/CMBS take-out**, and Morgan Stanley describes securitisation explicitly as "an increasingly sought-after take-out option". Construction and project loans are originated in private credit precisely because they are pre-stabilisation; once cash flows stabilise and the non-call period passes, the economics favour refinancing into public structured credit.

Morgan Stanley's July 2026 tracking shows the destination already forming: data-centre ABS is ~10% of commercial ABS and ~4% of the overall market at ~$45bn, CMBS ~4% of all CMBS at >$20bn, with **~$130bn of further ABS and CMBS issuance forecast from mid-2026** ([Morgan Stanley, *Data Center AI Financing: Investor Feedback*, pp. 21–22](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Corporate_and_Securitized_Credit_Data_Center_AI_Financing_Investor_Feedback-2026-07-13.pdf>)).

**A falling narrow weight is therefore ambiguous, not reassuring.** It is consistent with AI lending never materialising *and* with it materialising and migrating out on schedule. Only a look-through that spans private credit, ABS/CMBS and private IG together can tell the two apart — which is the same conclusion the public fixed income section reaches from the other direction.

One further coverage warning: Morgan Stanley's own AI-issuance tracker — **$217bn in FY2025, $336bn YTD 2026, $580bn forecast for FY2026** — is built from IG, HY, ABS, CMBS and leveraged loans. **It has no private-credit column at all** ([Exhibit 1, p. 2](<research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Corporate_and_Securitized_Credit_Data_Center_AI_Financing_Investor_Feedback-2026-07-13.pdf>)). The most-quoted AI debt-issuance series in the market is silent on the channel that the same house forecasts will be the largest.

## 4. Evidence, assumptions and remaining gaps

- **The entire narrow measurement rests on sources not held in this workspace.** The 8%, the >$200bn, the >$40bn of originations, the 4.7-year maturity and the $300–600bn projection all come from BIS notes available only as external links. They were not verified line-by-line here. For an IC paper this is a material dependency and should be closed by obtaining the three BIS publications before circulation. The same applies to the OECD, FSB, IMF and Federal Reserve citations.
- **One figure is carried forward unverified, but it survives a consistency test.** The prior version's "AI originations were only about 4% of total direct-loan originations" appears nowhere in this workspace's independent evidence notes and could not be corroborated against the source. It is, however, consistent with the model's own structure: a $2.5tn book growing at 11.2% with 4.7-year run-off requires gross originations of $2,500bn x (0.112 + 1/4.7) = $812bn a year, against which the BIS's ">$40bn" of AI originations is **4.9%**. The figure is therefore retained as the flat-origination row of the §3.3 table rather than discarded — but it should still be confirmed against the source, since no conclusion here rests on it.
- **The $D_0$ residual is an inference.** $730bn of the $2,500bn denominator is implied drawdown-fund leverage, not a published figure. It reconciles to a plausible 0.57×, corroborated by the BDC universe's observed 1.0× debt-to-equity, but a direct measurement would be better.
- **The numerator taxonomy is the binding limitation, not the denominator.** A borrower-vertical screen cannot see a data-centre SPV, a GPU-backed facility or a lease-financed compute portfolio. Every forward-looking source in the set expects those to dominate. This is why the narrow number is reported as narrow, and why §3.5 exists.
- **Cumulative gross financing is not loan stock.** The $800bn / $1tn / $1.4tn figures are three-to-five-year gross flows against a book with 4.7-year turnover and an active securitisation take-out. They cannot be added to today's $200bn, and §3.5 converts rather than adds.
- **J.P. Morgan's $1.4tn is not a credit number.** It spans "private credit/alternative capital" and the supporting deal list includes infrastructure equity. It is a capacity envelope.
- **Software is a screen, not a loss estimate.** AI benefits as well as disrupts software borrowers. The 19–26% must be decomposed by product substitutability, recurring-revenue quality, retention, leverage, cash interest coverage, maturity wall and sponsor support before losses are assigned.
- **BDC transparency creates sample bias.** BDCs file quarterly loan-level data and are the best observable proxy — the BIS uses exactly this route — but public BDCs, private BDCs, private-credit CLOs, infrastructure debt and insurer SMAs hold different books.
- **Valuation and liquidity amplify everything above.** Stale marks, PIK income and amendments delay recognition. Preqin reports BDC redemption requests "near or above their gates" in Q2 2026 ([*Private Credit Q2 2026*, p. 4](<research/Preqin-Private_Credit_Q2_2026_Quarterly_Update-2026-07-30.pdf>)); the IMF estimates selected perpetual non-traded BDC liquidity buffers could be exhausted in five to seven quarters under a moderate shock. Neither is an AI-concentration estimate; both determine how a shock is transmitted.
- **Scenario scores from the prior cross-asset output are excluded.** The 15% / 26% / 35% "dependency cases" and the associated 17–32% fund exposure and 64% stress-loss share were illustrative portfolio scores, not loan-stock measurements.

**Bottom line for the IC.** Report **~8% narrow AI exposure today rising to ~10% by 2030 (range 5–14%)**, a **separate 19–26% software-disruption screen**, and an **unmeasured AI-infrastructure sleeve with $0.8–1.4tn of forecast financing and no denominator**. Add origination share, unclassified exposure, top ultimate offtakers, HHI, and the same exposures as a percentage of NAV. The dashboard's job is to preserve the difference between measured stock, leading flow, calculated scenario and acknowledged blank — and in private credit the blank is currently the largest of the four.

## Cross-report answer to the research question

The reports support the central hypothesis, but with an important qualification: **economic exposure is broadening much faster than the underlying decision-making, financing obligations, and physical bottlenecks are diversifying.** Public-equity exposure now extends from hyperscalers and semiconductors into adopters, utilities, industrials, data-centre real estate, power, and grid equipment. Yet much of that broader set still depends on a small number of hyperscaler tenants, frontier chip suppliers, leading-edge foundries, power-constrained sites, and external financing channels.

### 1. Public-equity concentration is wider than a technology-sector label

Morgan Stanley's most useful starting point is its roughly 3,600-stock analyst map. It classifies each company as an enabler, adopter, both, protected, wildcard, disrupted, or unknown, then adds materiality, pricing power, proprietary-data advantage, workforce impact, and whether benefits arrive through revenue or costs. The mapping is refreshed about every six months, which is important because both exposure and materiality migrate. Its limitation is equally important: it is qualitative and deliberately does not estimate AI revenue, profit, or capex shares. Use it as a candidate universe, then apply quantitative haircuts. ([Mapping AI's Rate of Change](research/Morgan_Stanley-Mapping_AIs_Rate_of_Change_Where_the_Rubber_Meets_the_Road-2026-02-11.pdf), PDF pp. 50–51.)

J.P. Morgan supplies direct concentration diagnostics: a long-history Gini index for S&P 500 constituent weights, sector contributions to earnings growth, and constituent-versus-index volatility dispersion. Its Japan/Korea work shows how the answer changes by benchmark: a curated 50-name AI/semiconductor/data-centre cohort represented 33.7% of TOPIX 500 but 54.8% of Nikkei 225. These are strong templates for calculating AI-weighted benchmark exposure, HHI/effective names, and AI contribution to earnings growth rather than relying on headline sector weights. ([All the Rest Is No-AI](research/JP_Morgan-The_JP_Morgan_View_All_the_Rest_is_No_AI-2026-05-08.pdf), PDF pp. 6–8; [Japan/Korea Correlation](research/JP_Morgan-Japan_Equity_Strategy_Nikkei_225_KOSPI_Correlation_and_Outlook-2026-07-27.pdf), PDF pp. 2–4.)

The newly added fourth Morgan Stanley mapping makes the migration visible. Among more than 3,600 stocks, 412 companies with $8.7tn of market value moved up in AI exposure, 259 companies with $8.5tn moved up in materiality, and 102 companies with $2.4tn increased on both dimensions. Consumer, real estate, financials, and APAC showed the strongest broadening. Yet broadening did not eliminate value concentration: enabler/adopter stocks had added more than $14tn since end-2022, while stocks classed “Core to Thesis” added $11.6tn. Goldman Sachs provides the complementary macro constraint: AI-related company value gains reached roughly $27tn by June 2026 versus a $9tn baseline estimate of the present value of additional US capital revenues from AI. Even conservative attribution produced $14–17tn. The implication is that the universe can broaden while aggregate market value still embeds unusually optimistic adoption, profit-share, and persistence assumptions. ([Broadening Trend](research/Morgan_Stanley-Mapping_AIs_Rate_of_Change_The_Broadening_Trend_Continues-2025-07-20.pdf), PDF pp. 9–16 and 21; [Markets Ahead of the Macro](research/Goldman_Sachs-Global_Markets_Analyst_Where_the_AI_Boom_Stands_Now_Markets_Ahead_of_the_Macro-2026-06-22.pdf), PDF pp. 5–9.)

### 2. Credit concentration is rising faster in issuance flow than in outstanding index weight

This is the clearest cross-asset result. J.P. Morgan estimates that the wider AI ecosystem already represents 15.5%, or about $1.4tn, of the high-grade market and forecasts $2.1tn of high-grade borrowing over 2026–30. The HPC subsector rose from 1.07% of the high-yield index at end-2025 to 2.75%, with $350bn of forecast bond and loan issuance through 2030. Morgan Stanley separately shows why current credit benchmarks can understate the coming exposure: its 2025 financing bridge projected $2.9tn of data-centre spending through 2028, with $1.4tn funded by hyperscaler cash and the external gap distributed across IG bonds, ABS/CMBS, private credit, and PE/VC/sovereign capital. ([AI Capex 2.0](research/JP_Morgan-AI_Capex_2_0_If_You_Build_It_They_Will_Finance_It_Updated_Forecasts-2026-06-17.pdf), PDF pp. 32–37; [Bridging a $1.5tn Financing Gap](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Tech_Diffusion_and_GenAI_Bridging_a_1_5tr_Data_Center_Financing_Gap-2025-07-16.pdf), PDF pp. 7–10 and 27–34.)

The April 2026 Morgan Stanley fixed-income deck adds balance-sheet exposures that ordinary bond-index weights miss: more than $660bn of lease commitments across five hyperscalers and more than $640bn of disclosed purchase commitments linked to data centres. These obligations should sit beside issued bonds, loans, securitizations, and project debt in a look-through obligor map. ([Global Fixed Income Outlook](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf), PDF pp. 12–17.)

Morgan Stanley's August update shows how quickly the flow is scaling. It records $417bn of AI-related corporate-credit issuance year to date in 2026, including 4(a)(2) placements, versus $189bn in all of 2025; the total comprises $267bn of US IG, $62bn of non-US IG, $41bn of US HY, $10bn of leveraged loans, and $35bn of chip financing. Yet current index weight does not imply imminent saturation. AI-linked IG factor exposure had moved only a little above 6%; a 15% historical-sector threshold implies roughly $700bn of incremental capacity, while a largest-issuer comparison suggests about $796bn of hyperscaler capacity. The hierarchy of risk matters as much as the quantity: the report prefers amortizing, collateral-backed chip and stabilized data-centre debt to unsecured risk, and finds IG data-centre bonds trading 50–120bp wider than tenant unsecured debt and HY data-centre debt 100–230bp wider. ([A Break from the Heat, For Now](research/Morgan_Stanley-US_Credit_Strategy_A_Break_from_the_Heat_For_Now-2026-08-14.pdf), PDF pp. 2–11.)

The practical implication is to report both **stock exposure** and **flow exposure**. New issues, project SPVs, leases, guarantees, private placements, construction loans, and takeout securitizations should be mapped back to the ultimate tenant or guarantor; otherwise several wrappers around the same hyperscaler or site will appear diversified.

### 3. Private-credit AI exposure is two-sided

The folder contains useful private-credit evidence, but it is concentrated in two very different channels. The positive channel is asset-based and project financing of chips, powered shells, data centres, and energy. The negative channel is legacy software disruption. Morgan Stanley estimates software exposure at 26% for public BDCs, 19% for private-credit CLOs, 16% for US loans, 15% for broadly syndicated-loan CLOs, about 10% for European loans/CLOs, and roughly 3–3.5% for US and European HY. Much of the loan exposure is private, sponsor-backed, lower-rated, concentrated in weaker vintages, and faces a front-loaded maturity wall. This is a strong warning that a single unsigned “AI weight” is misleading: some holdings benefit from the buildout while others are exposed to disruption and refinancing risk. ([Mapping Software Exposure in Leveraged Credit](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Credit_and_CLO_Strategy_Mapping_Software_Exposure_in_Leveraged_Credit-2026-02-09.pdf), PDF pp. 4–5, 10–18, and 21.)

The added fixed-income deck corroborates those weights and sharpens the timing: 85% of software-loan issuers are private, more than half of software loans are rated B− or lower, the sub-18-month refinancing window begins rising from August 2026, maturities step up to about $60bn in 2028, and median direct-lending software leverage is about 8.0×. ([Global Fixed Income Outlook](research/Morgan_Stanley-Global_Fixed_Income_Outlook_April_2026-2026-04-13.pdf), PDF pp. 19–22.)

Preqin adds the market denominator and an observable route into the opacity. Q2 2026 private-credit fundraising reached a record $99bn—nearly three times Q1—but only 36 funds closed, the fewest since 2014; North America attracted $70bn and direct lending nearly three-quarters of capital. About 70% of aggregate target capital sat in funds seeking more than $1bn, while redemption gates at non-traded BDCs highlighted the difference between reported NAV and available liquidity. BDCs are especially useful for exposure mapping because they publish quarterly fair values and loan-level holdings, usually hold first-lien or unitranche floating-rate loans to sponsor-backed middle-market companies, and constrain leverage to roughly 2× debt/equity. At end-2025, 188 BDC vehicles represented more than $500bn of gross asset value and $250bn of NAV, with private/non-traded BDCs accounting for more than two-thirds of NAV. ([Private Credit Q2 2026](research/Preqin-Private_Credit_Q2_2026_Quarterly_Update-2026-07-30.pdf), PDF pp. 2–6; [BDCs Examined](research/Preqin-Private_Markets_Unpacked_BDCs_Examined-2026-07-08.pdf), PDF pp. 2–7.)

### 4. The next three to five years broaden the factor into power, industrials, infrastructure, and real estate

The reports consistently identify power and time-to-power as the most important physical bottlenecks. Useful forward variables include executable rather than announced MW, grid and interconnection status, transformer/turbine lead times, named tenant commitments, PPA or lease terms, cooling and rack density, and the share of onsite generation. Bloom's developer surveys show power is the leading site-selection constraint and that developers often prefer onsite generation to relocation when grid capacity is unavailable. J.P. Morgan's US project work demonstrates why announced pipelines need severe stage-based haircuts: hundreds of GW can be proposed while only a small fraction is contracted, cleared, or under construction. ([Bloom 2026 Power Report](research/Bloom_Energy-2026_Data_Center_Power_Report_The_New_Realities_Shaping_AI_Buildout_Mid_Year_Pulse-2026-06-22.pdf), PDF pp. 2, 7, and 11–13; [Data-Centre Demand Updates](research/JP_Morgan-Hardware_and_Networking_Key_Takeaways_on_Data_Center_Demand-2026-07-27.pdf), PDF pp. 1–2.)

This broadening can reduce name concentration while increasing common-factor concentration. A portfolio may own more securities across more sectors, yet become more dependent on the same compute-demand, hyperscaler-capex, power-availability, and financing assumptions.

The 2025 Morgan Stanley mapping provides leading indicators for that broadening: 32% of REITs moved up in AI exposure; insurance adopters rose from 48% to 71%; financial-services adopters rose from 66% to 73%; and APAC recorded a net 104 exposure upgrades. Across 162 public REIT and commercial-real-estate-services companies, it estimated a $34bn AI-driven labor-cost opportunity against $92bn of labor costs, equivalent to roughly 16% of operating cash flow before labor and interest. These are potential operating sensitivities rather than realized earnings, so they should be kept separate from contracted infrastructure exposure. ([Broadening Trend](research/Morgan_Stanley-Mapping_AIs_Rate_of_Change_The_Broadening_Trend_Continues-2025-07-20.pdf), PDF pp. 9 and 13–16.)

Preqin's 2030 forecasts put scale around the private-market broadening. Alternatives AUM is expected to exceed $30tn by 2030; private credit reaches about $4.5tn from $2.1tn in 2024, infrastructure about $2.9tn, and private equity about $11.8tn. The report explicitly links venture capital to AI-enabled startup formation and infrastructure to more than 100% growth in data-centre power demand by 2030, but these are total-market forecasts rather than AI-attributed AUM. The Q2 infrastructure update adds a useful concentration warning: 731 funds sought $463bn, and funds targeting at least $3bn were less than 10% of funds by number but more than half of capital sought. Digital infrastructure took an outsized share of capital, while 2022-vintage net IRRs ranged from 21.1% at the upper quartile to 5.3% at the lower quartile. Broadening into private infrastructure can therefore reduce listed-name concentration while adding manager, valuation, and execution concentration. ([Private Markets in 2030](research/Preqin-Private_Markets_in_2030-2025-10-10.pdf), PDF pp. 3, 6–8, 12–31; [Infrastructure Q2 2026](research/Preqin-Infrastructure_Q2_2026_Quarterly_Update-2026-08-13.pdf), PDF pp. 2–11.)

### 5. Conventional risk models miss part of the common AI factor

Morgan Stanley's Quant Matters papers provide the most reusable statistical method. They construct analyst-mapped thematic portfolios, strip out broad-market effects, estimate stock theme betas, and add thematic factors to a Barra-style style/country/industry framework. AI is the dominant axis in the residual thematic PCA, while adding themes can reclassify risk previously labelled company-specific—especially in concentrated winners and narrative-driven periods. The method is currently equity-centric, but the same AI-capex shock factor can be used with bond excess returns, CDS, listed infrastructure/REIT proxies, and carefully unsmoothed private-asset fundamentals. ([Beyond Traditional Factors](research/Morgan_Stanley-Quant_Matters_Beyond_Traditional_Factors_Enhancing_Portfolio_Risk_Attribution-2025-10-20.pdf), PDF pp. 2–17; [Global Thematic Landscape](research/Morgan_Stanley-Quant_Matters_Mapping_the_Thematic_Landscape_and_Risk_Attribution-2026-02-20.pdf), PDF pp. 3–17.)

Goldman Sachs adds an important decomposition for stress design: **aggregate shocks** change the total AI value available to US companies, while **distributional shocks** reallocate that value among hyperscalers, semiconductors, power suppliers, adopters, consumers, and non-US producers. Across five illustrative events, aggregate shocks lowered the S&P 500, widened credit spreads, and usually pushed Treasury yields lower; distributional shocks produced large offsetting rotations beneath a nearly flat index and had little rates impact. A portfolio-level AI factor should therefore contain at least two shocks—a total-value shock and a winner-share shock—because only the former is reliably hedgeable with broad indices and macro assets. ([Size of the Pie vs. Share of the Pie](research/Goldman_Sachs-Global_Markets_Comment_Distinguishing_AI_Risks_Size_of_the_Pie_vs_Share_of_the_Pie-2026-07-23.pdf), PDF pp. 4–11.)

Preqin's hedge-fund analysis supplies independent evidence that strategy labels do not remove the common factor. Regressing January 2024–June 2026 hedge-fund returns on global equities, global bonds, and the excess return of the STOXX Global AI Index over MSCI ACWI, it finds positive AI sensitivity in every top-level strategy; equity hedge funds have the highest sensitivity at 17.9%, implying about 0.18% of excess return for each 1% of AI-index outperformance. Software and semiconductor exposures were near historical highs, while statistical arbitrage returned 5.75% in Q2. The short sample and overlapping equity exposure make the estimates preliminary, but the method should be replicated on manager returns to identify hidden AI crowding across ostensibly diversified mandates. ([Hedge Funds Q2 2026](research/Preqin-Hedge_Funds_Q2_2026_Quarterly_Update-2026-07-24.pdf), PDF pp. 2–7.)

### 6. What this PDF set does not yet answer

No report completes a security- and asset-level pension portfolio aggregation across public equity, public credit, private credit, private equity, infrastructure, and real estate. Preqin improves the private-market denominator and BDC observability, but the main evidence gaps remain manager-level private-equity holdings, transaction-level data-centre real-estate exposure, consistent public/private debt identifiers, look-through positions in non-traded vehicles, and point-in-time global benchmark membership. The private-equity and real-estate quarterlies describe fundraising and deal concentration but do not isolate AI. The sell-side set provides the building blocks, not the final cross-asset concentration number.

### High-relevance reports

#### 1. Bloom Energy — 2026 Data Center Power Report

**File:** [Bloom_Energy-2026_Data_Center_Power_Report_The_New_Realities_Shaping_AI_Buildout_Mid_Year_Pulse-2026-06-22.pdf](research/Bloom_Energy-2026_Data_Center_Power_Report_The_New_Realities_Shaping_AI_Buildout_Mid_Year_Pulse-2026-06-22.pdf)

**Relevance:** High — Survey-based evidence on the power, permitting, community, emissions, and architecture constraints that can shift AI exposure into utilities and physical infrastructure.

**Angles:** infrastructure/power/real estate; public equity; cross-asset/common factor; 3–5y scenario.

**Key findings.** Power remains the main gating factor for data-center growth, but construction cost, equipment and skilled-labor availability, permitting, community opposition, emissions, water use, and electrical architecture are becoming a joint constraint set. The buildout is still expected to be prolonged: developers allocate 19%, 22%, 23%, 23%, and 13% of planned capacity additions to 2026–30 respectively. Inference already represents more than half of AI compute, earlier than prior expectations, suggesting demand is broadening from model training to persistent application use. When grid power is constrained, 61% of surveyed developers prefer onsite power and only 12% would relocate; about one-third of US sites are expected to be fully onsite-powered by 2030. This creates investable enabling exposure in generation, fuel cells, storage, CCUS, grid equipment, cooling, and specialized construction, but also political and stranded-project risk.

**Data/method/approach.** Bloom commissioned double-blind surveys in Apr-2024 (n=30), Nov-2024 (64), Apr-2025 (100), Nov-2025 (152), and Apr-2026 (156); the latest sample covers hyperscalers, colocation, neocloud, developer, and chip firms, with 79% US-based, supplemented by industry interviews and public announcements (PDF p. 2). External 2030 US power-demand forecasts span roughly 700–1,200 TWh; Morgan Stanley’s shown estimate is 755 TWh, SemiAnalysis 1,167 TWh, and S&P Global 1,069 TWh (p. 4). Site-selection results put power availability first (51% rank it top-three), ahead of fiber (32%), land (30%), and regulation (30%) (p. 7). Among onsite-powered sites, 31% are expected to use CCUS by 2030 and 41% by 2035 (p. 11). Rack density has risen from 25–40 kW to ~150 kW, with ~300 kW next and designs approaching 1 MW; hybrid/DC-native designs are forecast to reach 58% of new deployments by 2030, with DC-native alone at 36% (pp. 12–13). For the research model, use these as scenario drivers and haircuts on announced capacity—not as a security-level exposure measure.

#### 2. Deutsche Bank — Chips: The Biggest AI Bottleneck

**File:** [Deutsche_Bank-Chips_The_Biggest_AI_Bottleneck-2026-05-19.pdf](research/Deutsche_Bank-Chips_The_Biggest_AI_Bottleneck-2026-05-19.pdf)

**Relevance:** High — Maps extreme geographic and corporate chokepoints in advanced semiconductors and the likely broadening from GPUs into memory, networking, power management, and reshoring capex.

**Angles:** public equity; infrastructure; common factor; 3–5y scenario.

**Key findings.** The AI semiconductor chain is structurally concentrated but not in a single country: US firms dominate design software/IP, Taiwan leading-edge fabrication, the Netherlands EUV lithography, Korea/Japan memory and materials, and China/Southeast Asia packaging and mature-node production. This is “mutually assured dependence,” not self-sufficiency. Taiwan produces over 90% of the most advanced chips; ASML’s roughly 40–50 EUV systems per year cap frontier capacity, with about 3.5 EUV machines needed per GW of AI data-center capacity (PDF pp. 8, 11). TSMC’s earlier flat capex and 2–3-year fab lead times imply advanced-compute supply may not catch AI demand until 2028–29 (p. 13). The public-equity AI factor should therefore include non-US foundries, lithography, HBM/memory, packaging, power management, networking, and fab-equipment suppliers—but exposure is concentrated by chokepoint, not broad sector weight.

**Data/method/approach.** DB builds a supply-chain/geopolitical map from company disclosures, Bloomberg, trade and government sources. The semiconductor market is forecast at ~$1tn revenue in 2026 and >$1.5tn in 2030, versus ~$791bn in 2025 (p. 3). China is projected to hold 35–40% of mature-node capacity by 2027 (p. 11), while US chip-production share fell from ~40% in 1990 to ~11% in 2019 (p. 14). A Taiwan blockade is estimated elsewhere at $5tn first-year global losses and potentially an 11% US GDP decline—useful as a severe common-factor stress, not a base case (p. 13). China reportedly has ~2.8m H100-equivalent GPUs accessible domestically/remotely but remains constrained; leading US chips are estimated ~5x more powerful than Chinese alternatives, potentially widening to 17x (pp. 18–19). The next demand broadening is from GPUs into ASICs, networking, memory, and power management; however, efficiency news can cause violent correlated repricing, and actual HBM order cancellations are a better fundamental signal than headlines (pp. 21–22).

Aggregate shocks raise implied correlation and index volatility; distributional shocks can leave the index calm while single-stock dispersion remains high (pp. 9–11).

**Data / method / approach.** The report classifies AI narratives by whether they alter the total US corporate value pool or its distribution, then compares equities, rates, credit, volatility, FX, commodities, and Goldman macro-shock measures across five dated events. The sample is illustrative, selected, and too small for causal inference. Its value is as a scenario-design template: broad equity indices and rates can hedge aggregate AI risk, whereas concentrated AI portfolios require position-specific or relative-value hedges for distributional risk.

# Cross-Asset AI Concentration: Measurement Framework, Current Evidence and a 3–5 Year Forward View

Research report prepared August 2026. All data reflect the latest published sources available as at 18 August 2026.

Client research notes cited by title and date only are not linked.

## Executive summary

**Derived concentration, this report: US IG look-through is 6% today and 11% in 2030. Five-name unsecured is 4% today and 9% in 2030. Lease-adjusted economic exposure on the same five names is already 11% today. HY build-out is 3% today and 6% in 2030. A diversified pension book is 25% AI-weighted today and 29% in 2029/30. In an AI capex-reset stress, about 64% of modelled portfolio loss still comes through that channel.** Those are this report's numbers, derived below from J.P. Morgan, Apollo, Morgan Stanley and the Bank of England. They are not a reprint of any one house, and the rows are not additive.

The most decisive single data point comes from the Bank of England: the five AI hyperscalers accounted for **3% of outstanding US dollar investment-grade debt at end-2025 but over 15% of year-to-date dollar IG issuance by early May 2026**, while AI companies represented **1% of the JP Morgan high-yield index at end-2025 but 41% of non-refinancing US high-yield issuance in 2026 to date** ([Bank of England, Financial Stability Report, July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)). The same stock-versus-flow divergence appears in private credit, where AI-related deals rose to **34% of private-credit deal activity in 2025 from a 17% five-year average**, even though AI-related loans are only about **8% of outstanding private-credit loan volumes** ([Financial Stability Board, *Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit*](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P060526.pdf); [BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf)).

Six further judgements follow from the evidence base:

1. **In public equities, AI exposure has already crossed the point where "sector diversification" is meaningless.** The Bank of England estimates AI companies account for **around half of the S&P 500, up from roughly a quarter in 2022**, and the S&P 500 is around half of global equity market capitalisation ([BoE FSR, July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)). JPMorgan's equivalent range is **40–45% of the S&P 500 depending on breadth of definition** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/12/tsmc-samsung-sk-hynixs-south-korea-taiwan-stocks-ai-chipmakers-concentration-risk.html)). The IMF found the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index for **two of six major equity markets above its 95th historical percentile** ([IMF, GFSR April 2026, Chapter 1](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).
2. **The concentration is not only American.** TSMC is **more than 40% of Taiwan's Taiex**, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together are **42.2% of the Kospi**, and Goldman Sachs estimates AI-related revenue exposure at **well over 80% for Taiwan and around 60% for Korea** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/12/tsmc-samsung-sk-hynixs-south-korea-taiwan-stocks-ai-chipmakers-concentration-risk.html)). MSCI's value-chain scoring ranks **Taiwan and the Netherlands ahead of the US** on index-weighted AI exposure ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/mapping-ai-exposure-across-global-markets)).
3. **Credit stock: 6% now, 11% in 2030 on the rebuildable IG look-through; 3% now, 6% in 2030 on HY build-out.** Five-name unsecured is 4% now and 9% in 2030. Adding S&amp;P lease and guarantee adjustments already takes the same five names to about 11% economic exposure today, so the 2030 index print is catching up to a look-through that already exists. Flow is a different object: five names are already more than 15% of USD IG issuance and HPC is 41% of non-refi HY ([BoE FSR, July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)). Derivation is in §3.
4. **A large and growing share of AI credit risk is deliberately located outside both corporate balance sheets and bond indices.** The BIS labels hyperscalers' joint ventures and SPVs "**shadow borrowing**": obligations "economically akin to debt but largely residing outside corporate balance sheets", funded by private credit funds and insurers with bank funding lines behind them ([BIS Quarterly Review, March 2026](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603u.htm)).
5. **There are two opposite AI exposures in credit, and they must be scored separately.** AI *build-out* exposure (hyperscalers, data centres, neoclouds, power) sits alongside AI *disruption* exposure (SaaS borrowers). SaaS loans reached **over $500bn, 19% of total direct loans at end-2025**, from almost $8bn in 2015 ([BIS Quarterly Review box](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)); software is roughly **12% of syndicated CLO collateral and about 19% of direct lending** ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/debt-investors-offloading-exposure-software-companies-is-latest-sign-pain-2026-03-17/)). A portfolio can be long the AI capex cycle in one sleeve and short AI-driven margin destruction in another, with both losing in the same quarter — as happened in Q1 2026.
6. **Private markets are where measured exposure most understates economic exposure, and MSCI has now quantified the cross-asset overlap.** MSCI identified **$122bn of data-centre exposure across 584 companies and assets held by closed-end infrastructure, real-estate and private-equity funds as at Q3 2025**, with 60% ($73.3bn) in real assets and 36% in equity strategies, warning that "**while data-centre exposure spans multiple asset classes, allocations are anchored to the same driver — computational demand — meaning that apparent diversification may not fully mitigate thematic concentration risk**" ([MSCI research, via Real Asset Insight](https://realassetinsight.com/2026/04/17/data-centres-outperformed-all-asset-types-but-future-remains-uncertain-msci-research/)).

Applying the framework in this report to an illustrative diversified pension portfolio produces an **AI-weighted exposure of roughly 17–32% of assets (mid case ~25%)**, but an **AI-attributable share of about 64% of total portfolio loss in an AI capex-reset stress**. That gap — between weight and sensitivity — is the correct headline risk metric.

## 1. Defining and scoring AI exposure

### 1.1 Why a binary classification fails

The evidence shows AI exposure arriving through at least seven distinct economic channels: equity valuation, corporate earnings, capex demand, debt issuance and refinancing, collateral value, contracted cash flows, and power-market economics. No single classification captures these. Three specific failures matter for a pension fund:

- **Sector labels mis-sort the build-out.** Vanguard's "AI scalers" definition deliberately spans Software & Services, Technology Hardware, Semiconductors *and Electric Utilities* ([Vanguard, *AI exuberance*](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/isg_vemo_2026.pdf)). The IMF's AI ecosystem sample spans **73 public and private firms across nine jurisdictions**, split into "builders and energizers" (22 firms), chip developers (12) and data managers/data centres/GPU-neoclouds (22) ([IMF GFSR April 2026](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).
- **Legal entity ≠ economic obligor.** Data-centre SPVs, JVs, ABS master trusts and lease-backed high-yield structures separate the borrower of record from the economic sponsor and the ultimate offtaker ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603u.htm); [Structured Finance Association](https://structuredfinance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/SFA-Research-Corner_How-Data-Center-ABS-and-CMBS-Fit-in-a-Broader-Financing-Ecosystem.pdf)).
- **The same "AI" label hides opposite signs.** Aware Super, which found AI thematic exposure of around 15% of its high-growth option and "likely even larger" at whole-of-portfolio level, explicitly notes that the same theme has different risk profiles depending on "attachment points" and "positions in the capital structure", so simple dollar aggregation is insufficient ([Investment Magazine](https://www.investmentmagazine.com.au/2026/07/aware-super-hunts-hidden-ai-exposures-as-concentration-concern-grows/)).

### 1.2 The three-tier taxonomy

| Tier | Definition | Constituents evidenced in 2026 sources | Indicative dependency score |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Direct** | Revenue, assets or contracted cash flows are predominantly AI compute | Hyperscalers (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle per [BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603u.htm)); chip designers and foundries; memory; AI-model developers (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI); neoclouds (CoreWeave, Crusoe, IREN, Lambda per [NBER w35290](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290)); wholesale/colocation data centres | 75–100 |
| **Tier 2 — Enabling** | Supplies a constrained physical input to AI capacity; earnings tied to build-out volumes | Power generation and grid, electrical equipment, cooling, networking, construction/engineering, fibre, specialised materials (gallium: China supplies ~99% of refined output per [IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)) | 25–85, evidence-weighted |
| **Tier 3 — Indirect / second-order** | Growth, financing capacity, valuation or collateral value materially linked to AI capex or AI adoption | Insurers writing data-centre construction cover ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/06/ai-data-centers-financing-insurance-deals-gpu-debt.html)); land and industrial real estate near transmission; regional banks lending to SPVs; **and AI-disruption-negative** exposures such as SaaS borrowers | −40 to +40 (signed) |

The critical design choice is that Tier 3 is **signed**. A software borrower whose revenue model is threatened by AI has high AI *sensitivity* with a negative sign, which is precisely what the Q1 2026 experience demonstrated: software stocks fell almost **30% between October 2025 and February 2026**, BDC share prices fell about **10%**, and BDCs with high software exposure underperformed low-exposure peers by around **5 percentage points** ([BIS Quarterly Review box](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)).

### 1.3 The scoring function

For each security, borrower or asset *i*, construct an AI dependency score \(AI_i \in [-100, 100]\) as an evidence-weighted composite:

\[
AI_i = w_R R_i + w_C C_i + w_K K_i + w_H H_i + w_F F_i - w_D D_i
\]

where \(R_i\) is AI-linked revenue or contracted-cash-flow share; \(C_i\) is AI-linked capex, order book or pipeline share; \(K_i\) is criticality/substitutability in the value chain; \(H_i\) is counterparty concentration to hyperscalers, neoclouds or AI-native firms; \(F_i\) is financing dependence on continued AI-related capital-market access; and \(D_i\) is AI-disruption vulnerability of the business model. Suggested starting weights for equities are 0.30/0.20/0.15/0.15/0.05/0.15, and for credit 0.20/0.15/0.10/0.25/0.20/0.10 — credit weights the counterparty and refinancing channels more heavily because that is where losses arise.

Two disciplines make this defensible rather than arbitrary:

- **Evidence tiering.** Each input carries a confidence grade: (A) company-disclosed quantitative segment data; (B) third-party measured data such as leases, interconnection queues, order backlogs; (C) sector prior with a cap. MSCI's published approach — scoring companies across **10 components of the AI value chain using segment revenue and company news attention**, then reporting a concentration-adjusted score of *index-weighted AI score × (1 − HHI)* — is a usable external benchmark and cross-check ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/mapping-ai-exposure-across-global-markets)).
- **Sector caps.** No Grade-C sector prior may exceed the observed sector-level attribution (see §1.4). This prevents the "all utilities are AI" error.

### 1.4 Justifying the utilities and industrials haircut with observable data

Blanket haircuts are indefensible; attributions built from measurable AI load and spend are not. Four observable inputs now exist:

\[
AI_{\text{utility}} = \text{AI load share} \times \text{margin capture} \times \text{contract certainty} \times \text{incrementality}
\]

- **AI load share** is measurable at market level. Data centres accounted for **$6.3bn, or 38%, of charges in PJM's latest base capacity auction, and $29.4bn of $63.6bn (46%) across the last four auctions** ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/pjm-data-centers-capacity-auction-imm-bowring/825626/)). In the US, data centres account for **nearly half of electricity-demand growth to 2030** and over 20% of demand growth in advanced economies ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)).
- **Margin capture and incrementality** are visible in capital plans: Duke Energy **$103bn**, Southern Company **$81bn**, Dominion **$65bn**, Oncor **$47.5bn**, with large-load queues of **more than 75GW (Southern)** and **225GW (Oncor, about one-fifth of current US generation capacity)** ([Argus](https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2794366-us-utilities-boost-capex-plans-to-records-on-ai-demand)).
- **Contract certainty** must discount the queue heavily. Aurora Energy Research warns that "the more projects are asking to be interconnected, the more skeptical we have to be about them being realized", and that few forecasts account for load-shopping across multiple utilities ([Argus](https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/2794366-us-utilities-boost-capex-plans-to-records-on-ai-demand)). Dominion's counter-evidence is that forecast demand through 2045 is "more than covered by existing signed ESAs and CLOAs".
- **Delivery risk** caps scores further: the IEA estimates **around 20% of planned data-centre projects are at risk of delay** absent grid action, with transmission build times of four to eight years and doubled lead times for transformers and cables ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)).

A defensible calibration is therefore: diversified regulated utility with disclosed data-centre load but socialised rate base **10–25**; regulated utility with large contracted data-centre pipeline and incremental new-build **30–50**; merchant generator or developer with signed hyperscaler PPAs **50–80**. The same logic applies to electrical equipment and engineering firms, scored from disclosed data-centre order backlog share rather than sector membership.

### 1.5 Level of classification

Use three levels simultaneously rather than choosing one: **Level 1 (GICS sector)** for governance reporting and benchmark comparison only; **Level 2 (sub-industry, ~14 nodes)** as the primary measurement layer — chip design, semi equipment, foundry/memory, cloud/hyperscaler, AI model developers, software, networking, data centres, neocloud/GPU cloud, power generation, transmission and grid equipment, cooling, construction/engineering, AI adopters; **Level 3 (issuer, project, tenant, sponsor)** for material names and all private assets. Broad GICS is demonstrably too coarse: it puts a data-centre REIT and a shopping-centre REIT in the same bucket while separating a hyperscaler's equity from the SPV debt that finances its campus.

For a global benchmark, the arithmetic is straightforward and uncomfortable: with the US at roughly seven-tenths of MSCI World and AI at roughly half of the S&P 500, AI-linked market capitalisation is plausibly **~40%+ of MSCI World** before any Tier 2 enabling exposure outside the US is added. ### 2.2 Earnings and capex concentration, not just weight

Market-cap weight understates the dependency because earnings growth is even more concentrated. For Q2 2026, the Magnificent Seven's estimated earnings growth was **31.1%** versus 22.8% for the other 493, and excluding just Micron and Nvidia would cut the S&P 500's blended growth rate from **24.7% to 16.8%** ([FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/are-magnificent-7-companies-still-top-contributors-to-sp-500-earnings-growth-for-q2)). MSCI's cross-market work shows US AI-exposed companies increased capex by **nearly 60% in the year to May 2026, about ten times the 6% of non-AI peers, with revenue growth a fraction of that pace** — while in Taiwan and China AI capex and sales growth track each other closely, implying the "over-expenditure risk is, for now, a predominantly U.S. phenomenon" ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/mapping-ai-exposure-across-global-markets)).

Valuation dependence is equally concentrated. Before mid-2026 market stress, S&P 500 and Nasdaq EPS would have had to compound at close to **30% and 35% respectively** for P/Es to revert to 10-year medians by 2027 ([IMF GFSR April 2026](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)). The BoE notes that even **excluding the top 30 AI-related stocks**, the S&P 500 excess CAPE yield was around its lowest since 2007 — i.e. the AI trade has repriced the whole index, not just its AI members ([BoE FSR](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)).

### 2.3 Recommended equity metric set

1. **AI-weighted exposure**: \(\sum w_i AI_i\), reported for benchmark and portfolio, with low/mid/high scoring bands.
2. **Concentration**: HHI and effective number of names on AI-adjusted exposures; top-5 and top-10 AI-adjusted shares; MSCI-style concentration-adjusted score (score × (1 − HHI)) for cross-market comparison.
3. **Fundamental dependence**: AI contribution to index earnings growth, capex growth and market-cap growth (the FactSet and MSCI approaches above are replicable).
4. **Common-factor beta**: regress each holding on an AI factor (e.g. a rules-based AI basket, or the JP Morgan JPAMAIDE basket the BoE uses) controlling for market, size, value and sector.
5. **Geographic and value-chain decomposition**: exposure by AI node and by power market, not by country of listing.

Sell-side baskets — Morgan Stanley's enablers/adopters series, Goldman's phased AI baskets, the JPMorgan AI basket used by the Bank of England, MSCI's ACWI value-chain scores and the Bloomberg AI Index (top 45 cloud/semiconductor/hardware names per the [IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)) — should be used as **candidate universes and cross-checks only**. They are US-centric, momentum-influenced, and rebalance with the narrative rather than the economics.

### 3.1 Index stock already understates economic exposure

A technology print of about 10% of US IG, or a five-name hyperscaler print of 3–5%, is the visible corporate-bond residue of a financing system that has already moved off the unsecured index. Morgan Stanley's August 2026 "AI-level factor" for IG has only gone from below 3% to slightly over 6% this year — still small against banks' 26% historical peak — which is exactly why a governance report that stops at GICS or index stock will conclude the book is diversified (Morgan Stanley, A Break from the Heat, For Now, 14 August 2026; [Neuberger Berman](https://www.nb.com/insights/how-spacex-and-ai-spending-are-reshaping-investment-grade-credit)).

Four channels sit outside that print.

**Duration already exceeds market-value weight.** Amazon is the largest duration weight in US IG, from 20th a year earlier; Google is 18th, from 86th. About 40% of 15-year-plus IG issuance in 2026 is AI-related ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/goldman-sachs-exchanges/how-ai-debt-is-reshaping-the-credit-market)). Apollo puts AI-related IG at nearly 40% of longer-duration corporate supply ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)). A pension LDI or long-credit sleeve can therefore be more AI-exposed than its market-value weight implies before any look-through of SPVs.

**Legal issuer is not the economic obligor.** Morgan Stanley's 13 April 2026 Global Fixed Income Outlook already puts more than $660bn of leases and more than $640bn of disclosed data-centre purchase commitments across five hyperscalers — stock that does not sit in JULI or Bloomberg IG. The [BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603u.htm) labels the JV/SPV layer "shadow borrowing". Apollo identifies at least $90bn of contractual backstops — Google and Meta leases, Meta/Nvidia/AMD compute-as-a-service support, Broadcom residual-value support, an Oracle power guarantee — that have "supported tens of billions in associated debt" and "allow the credit strength of the largest technology companies to be transmitted across the AI value chain" ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)). Morgan Stanley's August note is more specific on ratings: S&P is projecting about $585bn of debt adjustments for the four high-quality hyperscalers (AMZN, META, GOOGL, MSFT) in 2027, mostly leases signed but not commenced, PPAs and guarantees. Reported leverage is therefore not the leverage that binds ratings or economic exposure.

**The same names now sit in HY, ABS and private IG under different labels.** Data-centre construction bonds, GPU loans, chip 4(a)(2) facilities and lease-backed HY notes are not "technology" in a GICS sense. They are offtaker risk plus construction, residual-value and power risk. Apollo's own exposure map runs from pure-play model builders through hyperscalers and off-balance-sheet SPV debt to power, DC operators, fibre, commodities and construction — and then asks the portfolio question directly: "How much AI exposure already exists within a portfolio? The answer is often larger than traditional sector labels suggest" ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)).

**Software is the opposite-sign sleeve, not a second build-out weight.** Morgan Stanley's February 2026 software map puts software at 16% / $235bn of US loans and 3.5% of ICE BofA HY. Apollo's near-term test is refinancing: more than $100bn of software and services loans mature between 2027 and 2029. CRWV, WULF and CIFR appear in both J.P. Morgan's HPC working list and Morgan Stanley's software HY list. Adding "AI credit" across those sleeves is the wrong object: one side is long the capex cycle, the other is short AI-driven displacement, and both can lose in the same quarter.

### 3.2 Concentration is already a flow fact

The credit-specific question is whether AI concentration is increasing faster in new borrowing than is visible in existing bond-index weights. It is. Outstanding weights are a lagging object; 2026 issuance is the leading one.

| Sleeve | Outstanding stock | 2026 flow | Why the gap matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five hyperscalers, USD IG | 3% at end-2025 ([BoE FSR, July 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)); 4.15% in JULI in June 2026 (J.P. Morgan, AI Capex 2.0) | More than 15% of YTD USD IG issuance by early May ([BoE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)); about one-third of net IG and nearly 40% of 10y+ supply ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)) | Stock will follow flow with a lag. A book that rebalances to the index is buying the concentration after it has been issued. |
| HPC / data-centre HY | 1.07% of JPM HY at YE2025, 2.75% by mid-June (J.P. Morgan, AI Capex 2.0); Apollo: virtually zero a year earlier | 41% of non-refinancing US HY ([BoE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)); Morgan Stanley: AI about 3% of HY now, with construction bonds a rising share | HY is being rewritten as a data-centre sector while the index weight still looks small. |
| Wider AI credit tape | Not an index weight | Morgan Stanley 14 August: $267bn US IG YTD across hyperscalers, DC construction, semis and REITs; $417bn including 4(a)(2) chip financing and $62bn of non-USD hyperscaler IG | Non-USD and private-placement flow already import the same names into global IG and insurance books. |

The 15–34% "share of IG issuance" range that circulates in 2026 is almost entirely definitional — five names versus AI-related, gross versus net — and none of it is an index weight ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/banks-get-creative-look-further-afield-ai-fueled-debt-soars-2026-06-29/); [Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/goldman-sachs-exchanges/how-ai-debt-is-reshaping-the-credit-market); [Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)). What is not definitional is the cash-flow driver behind the flow. Across the four high-quality hyperscalers, Morgan Stanley equity research implies a 57% increase in total capex into 2027 and a swing in free cash flow from about $20bn in 2026 to −$140bn in 2027 (Morgan Stanley, 14 August 2026). Incremental annual debt has already risen from 9% of capex in FY24 to 32% on an LTM basis by mid-2026 ([FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/hyperscalers-tap-external-financing-as-ai-capex-outruns-cash-flow)). Issuance can pause after a busy summer. The 12–18 month financing gap does not.

### 3.3 How the form of concentration changes

The path that matters for a pension book is not "hyperscalers go from 5% of IG to 10%". Apollo's Exhibit 27 already has that print — 2.7% in July 2025, 4.8% in July 2026, 8.8% by 2030 including SpaceX, "nearly 10% in the next four years" — and J.P. Morgan's five-name JULI weight is on the same trajectory ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf); J.P. Morgan, AI Capex 2.0). Direction is not the insight. The insight is that unsecured public IG is the sleeve that fills first, and that the residual financing changes the location and the legal wrapper of the same economic risk.

**Market capacity binds before balance-sheet capacity.** Morgan Stanley sizes about $400bn of remaining high-quality hyperscaler debt inside the current ratings band (combined adjusted net leverage still below S&P's 1.0x downgrade threshold through YE27, after ~$585bn of lease and other adjustments). Separately, IG could absorb another ~$700bn before an AI cohort approached the 14–15% historical non-financial sector peak, or ~$800bn if each of AMZN, MSFT, META, GOOGL and ORCL were taken to the 2.6% average largest-issuer share. Spreads are the release valve, not a stop: they have moved by multiple standard deviations, but not enough to constrain high-quality issuance (Morgan Stanley, 14 August 2026). Apollo draws the same distinction with different ceilings: the ecosystem could fundamentally support more than $2tn of additional IG debt, but public IG may absorb less than $1tn through 2030 because single-name, sector, spread-duration and factor-risk limits bind first. The rest is a private-IG problem of more than $1tn — private placements, infrastructure debt, ABS, equipment financings, project-level structures ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)). Goldman's analogue is $510bn of incremental US IG if hyperscalers are brought up to the largest US banks, against a theoretical $2tn of issuer capacity at 2x net leverage ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/goldman-sachs-exchanges/how-ai-debt-is-reshaping-the-credit-market)). The live disagreement is the public-IG ceiling, not the direction, and not whether leverage is the first constraint. It is not.

**What does not fit in unsecured HQ IG does not disappear.** It migrates. Morgan Stanley expects incremental capex to shift from data-centre shells toward chips and energy as ~20GW of HQ capacity comes online in 2026 and ~27GW in 2027. Compute lends itself to asset-level financing: the Broadcom-backed $35bn chip facility, Nvidia's compute-infrastructure platform with more than $500bn of third-party capital, GPU bonds and loans, and more Beignet-style structures. Private capital and high-quality semiconductor credit support grow; public IG issuance stays elevated because the market is deep, not because it is the whole stack (Morgan Stanley, 14 August 2026). Apollo's mapping is the same migration written from the private-IG side. For a look-through book this is the dangerous step. The unsecured index weight can look contained at 6–10% while the same offtakers backstop DC construction, ABS/CMBS, GPU residual-value paper and private IG. Morgan Stanley is explicit that private financing is not necessarily incremental investor capital: large managers allocate across public and private credit, and insurers are expanding private-capital relationships. Reallocation from public IG is not new money.

**HY is being rewritten as a build-out sector, and it is more dynamic than a static weight.** Apollo: direct data-centre exposure in HY has gone from virtually zero to nearly 5%, which as a standalone sector would be the sixth-largest HY constituent. Morgan Stanley: AI is about 3% of HY today, another ~$170bn would take it to a 15% illustrative cap, but construction bonds can migrate to ABS or IG once construction risk burns off, freeing HY capacity for the next vintage. Cost of capital is the stabilizer down the quality spectrum — coupons approaching ~10% begin to delay developer deals — in a way it is not for AA/AAA hyperscalers (Morgan Stanley, 14 August 2026). J.P. Morgan's HPC path of $80bn of leveraged finance in 2026 and $350bn of HPC bonds and loans in 2026–30 is the same sleeve viewed from the issuance side.

**Semiconductors have joined the credit ecosystem, which tightens the factor.** For most of 1H26 the credit narrative was hyperscalers in IG, neoclouds in HY, and data-centre construction in both. Morgan Stanley's August note is that Nvidia and Broadcom now trade increasingly in tandem with the hyperscalers, and that implicit credit support — residual-value guarantees, flexed AA ratings used to bring private credit into chip finance — matters more than Nvidia's own $25bn June issue. Component suppliers are becoming "strategic counterparties capable of underpinning large, capital-intensive AI infrastructure investments" (Morgan Stanley, 14 August 2026). A book that treated semis as a separate industrial factor is now holding a second claim on the same build-out.

**The opposite-sign wall arrives on the same calendar.** Apollo's software/services maturity wall of more than $100bn in 2027–29 coincides with the hyperscaler borrowing peak both Apollo and Goldman place in 2027, and with Morgan Stanley's 2027 FCF gap. A 2027–29 refinancing and supply cluster can therefore hit build-out credit and disruption credit in the same window.

### 3.4 Why sector and asset-class diversification fails

Apollo's Theme 3 is the central hypothesis of this report, stated from inside the credit market. Portfolios that appear diversified across sectors and asset classes are increasingly exposed to one driver: the pace of AI adoption and infrastructure spending. A book that holds hyperscaler bonds, data-centre debt, power infrastructure, semiconductor exposure, GPU-backed financings or AI-related equity "may appear diversified but contains assets highly related to the same underlying factor" ([Apollo](https://www.apollo.com/content/dam/apolloaem/documents/insights/2026/2026-midyear-credit-outlook.pdf)). Cloud, power and real estate, and the asset classes leveraged finance, equities and IG credit, "may therefore be more correlated than traditional classifications suggest."

That is the diversification failure a pension book should be testing. It is not tested by asking whether technology is 10% or 12% of IG. It is tested by asking whether IG corporates, HY, ABS, private credit, infrastructure and listed equity still have independent drivers once each position is scored by offtaker, power market and dependence on continued AI financing. Morgan Stanley's summer tape is early evidence that they do not: data-centre construction bonds widened in tandem with unsecured hyperscaler bonds, and semiconductor credit has begun to do the same. The house recommendation that follows — collateral-backed DC and GPU risk over unsecured HQ corporate risk — is a relative-value statement inside the factor, not an argument that the factor has split.

Two implications follow for measurement.

First, **do not add incompatible sleeves.** Five-name JULI, narrow HG, wide "AI ecosystem", HPC HY, software HY and private IG are different objects. J.P. Morgan's 15.5% / $1.4tn wide sleeve has no published constituent file. Apollo's 8.8% 2030 total includes SpaceX; JULI does not. Software 3.5% is disruption, not HPC.

Second, **report a metric set, not a blended AI %.**

- Market-value weight, by sleeve, with the definition printed next to the number.
- Spread-duration weight. This is already the larger number in long IG.
- Ultimate-obligor and offtaker look-through, aggregated across unsecured bonds, leases, SPVs, ABS/CMBS, GPU/chip facilities and private IG.
- Sign: build-out versus disruption.
- Refinancing profile by year, with 2027–29 flagged for both hyperscaler supply and the software loan wall.
- Stress sensitivity: spread, duration and expected-loss contribution under an AI capex or financing shock, which is the object §7 estimates at portfolio level.

The now-versus-path monitor is then a check on each sleeve against its own house path — five-name JULI toward the high-single digits, HPC HY toward the mid-single digits, public IG absorbing only part of the need, private IG and collateral taking the residual — not a single blended AI weight drifting from 5% to 10%.

## 4. Private markets

Private markets require estimation of **ranges, direction and concentration**, not false precision. The tiering below sets expectations by data availability.

| Evidence tier | What is measurable | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Loan/asset-level data (SMA, co-invest, BDC filings) | Borrower, sponsor, ultimate offtaker, collateral, LTV, leverage, maturity, power market | High |
| Manager-level disclosure | AI-related sector splits, data-centre/GPU/power lending, top borrowers and sponsors, portfolio overlap | Medium |
| Fund-level only | Strategy, vintage, deployment pace, peer benchmarks, observable deal activity | Low |
| No usable data | Strategy-based prior with explicit range and disclosure | Low — must be labelled |

### 4.1 Private credit

Private credit is the single most important private channel because it is being explicitly designed into the AI financing stack. Morgan Stanley's framework put **$2.9trn of global data-centre capex through 2028, of which ~$1.4trn funded by hyperscaler cash flow, $200bn corporate debt, $150bn ABS/CMBS, ~$800bn private credit and ~$350bn other capital** ([Morgan Stanley](https://www.morganstanley.com/content/dam/msdotcom/en/assets/pdfs/Research_Bridging-Data-Center-Gap.pdf)); the FSB cites the same **$2.9trn / $1.5trn external / $800bn private credit** decomposition ([FSB](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P060526.pdf)).

Measured exposure today is smaller than that pipeline implies, which is exactly the point:

- Outstanding private-credit loans to AI-related companies (PitchBook AI/Big Data/Cloud verticals) have risen from near zero to **over $200bn, almost 8% of total outstanding volumes**, with originations of **over $40bn in 2025** versus about $3bn in 2010; roughly **20% of funds** lend to AI-related sectors and AI loans are about **5% of volumes for the average fund** ([BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf)).
- AI-related deals were **34% of private-credit deal activity in 2025 versus a 17% five-year average**, and the BIS projects **$300–600bn of outstanding private credit to AI firms by 2030** ([FSB](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P060526.pdf); [BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf)).
- Pricing does not differentiate: AI loans average **$169m versus $90m** in size but carry spreads of **6.2pp versus 6.1pp** and similar maturities, a gap the BIS calls a "schism" implying either lenders underestimate risk or equity markets overestimate cash flows ([BIS Bulletin 120](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull120.pdf)).
- The mirror-image exposure is larger and older: **SaaS loans reached over $500bn, 19% of direct loans, at end-2025, with a third of private-credit funds lending to SaaS** ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)); BDCs have lent about **$115bn to software firms — a fifth of all BDC lending and over 80% of their technology portfolios** — and had not priced software exposure differently as of July 2026 ([BIS Bulletin 128](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull128.htm)). The IMF notes exposure to software borrowers "may exceed 50% of NAV (equity) for leveraged private credit vehicles" ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).
- Liquidity structure amplifies both: semi-liquid vehicles are about **20% of private-credit net assets** ($425bn gross / $241bn net across perpetual BDCs and interval funds), redemption requests rose in Q4 2025 and accelerated in Q1 2026, and the IMF estimates buffers would be exhausted after **five to seven quarters** under a moderate shock ([Federal Reserve, FSR May 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20260508.pdf); [IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).

**Practical measurement.** Use listed BDC filings as the highest-frequency observable proxy — the BIS uses exactly this route because BDCs are publicly traded, file quarterly and represent about a fifth of US direct loans ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)). Supplement with a mandatory manager AI look-through schedule covering: data centres; GPUs/compute; hyperscaler and neocloud offtakers; power/grid projects serving data centres; AI-native software; SaaS borrowers by rating and maturity; and **top-10 ultimate offtakers aggregated across all vehicles**. Ask specifically about double-pledged collateral, which the IMF flags among weaknesses exposed by recent credit failures.

### 4.2 Private equity

Private equity carries a two-sided AI exposure that is unusually easy to under-report.

- **Concentration through the largest rounds.** PE-backed unicorn rounds of $1bn+ totalled **$179.33bn in Q1 2026, nearly 86% of all AI funding round value**, versus 55% for full-year 2025 and about 41% in 2024, led by OpenAI's **$122bn** February round and xAI's **$20bn** Series E ([S&P Global Market Intelligence](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2026/6/concentration-risk-rises-as-private-equity-pours-billions-into-ai-103088317)). Broader venture data corroborate: **$407bn raised by AI startups in H1 2026 across 3,500 transactions — more than all of 2025's $264.1bn — with horizontal platforms taking 70.8% of capital and venture-growth valuations up 204% year on year** ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q2-2026-ai-report-407-billion-raised-as-megadeals-dominate)).
- **The LP aggregation problem, stated explicitly.** Because GPs underwrite independently, "instead of taking a 10% allocation to this thing, [an LP may] have 15%, 20%" exposure across managers; Market Intelligence describes overlapping exposure to the same category leaders as "a structural reality worth mapping" ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2026/6/concentration-risk-rises-as-private-equity-pours-billions-into-ai-103088317)).
- **The legacy software book is the negative-sign exposure.** US software deal value was **$16.24bn in the first five months of 2026, an annualised pace of roughly a quarter of 2025's record $156bn**; platform deals fell to **41% of software deal value from 71% a year earlier**; and the Medallia restructuring — a $6.4bn 2021 take-private that could cost Thoma Bravo as much as **$5.1bn** — shows how quickly sponsor equity can be wiped out when AI narratives, valuations and credit conditions move together ([Reuters/PitchBook via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/pe-pivots-platform-buyouts-software-221430634.html)). [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-08/ai-fears-spur-70-plunge-in-private-equity-tech-deal-value) reported AI fears driving a **70% plunge in PE tech deal value** in mid-2026.
- **Manager behaviour is adapting, which creates measurement drift.** McKinsey reports GPs re-underwriting existing portfolios asset-by-asset for AI, with **6% of GPs currently seeing high AI impact on their own operations versus 70% expecting it in three to five years** ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/private-capital/our-insights/global-private-markets-report/private-equity)).

### 4.3 Infrastructure

Infrastructure has become a partially AI-driven asset class:

- **Data centres were nearly 21% of Q1 2026 infrastructure deal value** ($85bn total, down ~25% quarter on quarter), with deployment "anchored in energy and digital infrastructure" ([Preqin](https://www.preqin.com/insights/research/quarterly-updates/infrastructure-q1-2026-preqin-quarterly-update)).
- Fundraising capacity is large and concentrated: a record **$221bn raised by private infrastructure funds in 2025** at an average fund size of $1.8bn, **$1.7trn+ AUM and nearly $400bn of dry powder as at September 2025**, and **695 funds seeking $555bn as at May 2026**; Goldman sees potential for **$3trn+ AUM by 2030** ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/private-markets-expected-to-have-growing-role-in-data-center-financing)).
- Deal sizes are systemically large: private infrastructure data-centre deals were consistently above $10bn in 2025, including the **$40bn Aligned Data Centers acquisition** by a consortium including Nvidia, Microsoft, BlackRock and xAI ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/06/ai-data-centers-financing-insurance-deals-gpu-debt.html); [Knight Frank](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2026/7/quantifying-technology-in-real-estate-2026-data-centres)).
- Power assets are the second AI leg. Data centres are responsible for **38–46% of recent PJM capacity-auction charges** ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/pjm-data-centers-capacity-auction-imm-bowring/825626/)), tech accounted for about **40% of all corporate renewable PPAs signed in 2025**, and the conditional SMR offtake pipeline with data-centre operators grew from **25GW at end-2024 to 45GW** ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions)).

Exposure should be estimated from **committed capital by vintage, transaction volumes by sub-sector, contracted capacity (MW) with offtaker identity, and project pipelines discounted for interconnection risk** — with the IEA's 20%-of-projects-delayed estimate as a standing haircut.

### 4.4 Real estate

Real estate has quietly become one of the most AI-levered private allocations at the margin:

- **Data centres were 31% of global private real-estate fundraising in Q1–Q3 2025, versus a 15% average since 2020**, with two of the ten largest funds closed in 2025 being data-centre vehicles ([Colliers/PERE via DatacenterDynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/data-centers-account-for-nearly-a-third-of-global-real-estate-investment-in-2025-report/)).
- Fundamentals are extraordinary and therefore fragile: North American absorption hit **25GW in H1 2026 (double a year earlier)**, vacancy has been **1% for a third consecutive year**, **66GW is under construction of which 95% is pre-committed**, and rents are up nearly **70% since 2020** ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-us/newsroom/data-center-demand-exceeds-expectations-in-h1-2026)). Global vacancy across the 16 largest markets fell to **6.7% from 8.3%**, with Northern Virginia at **0.3%** ([CBRE](https://www.cbre.com/press-releases/global-data-center-supply-demand-scarcity-available-space)).
- Tenant concentration is the exposure: **hyperscalers are 59% of 2026 demand, neoclouds 11% and pure-play AI companies 7%** ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-us/newsroom/data-center-demand-exceeds-expectations-in-h1-2026)). The IMF lists hyperscaler tenant concentration, obsolescence, speculative timing and grid delays as the sector's core vulnerabilities, with interconnection lead times of **two to seven years** ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).
- Land and power now drive value: sites able to deliver **50MW+ grid connections within three years command up to three-to-four times traditional industrial land values**, and grid capacity across core metros is largely reserved through 2030 ([Knight Frank](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2026/7/quantifying-technology-in-real-estate-2026-data-centres)).

### 4.5 The cross-asset overlap is now measured

MSCI's private-capital analysis is the closest thing to direct proof of the report's central hypothesis: **$122bn of data-centre exposure spread across infrastructure (real assets 60%, $73.3bn), private equity strategies (36%) and real estate**, spanning five categories from hyperscalers to supporting software — all anchored to a single driver, computational demand, with data centres returning **23.1% annualised since 2011 versus 18.1% for global PE, 12.1% for infrastructure and 10.1% for public equities** ([MSCI via Real Asset Insight](https://realassetinsight.com/2026/04/17/data-centres-outperformed-all-asset-types-but-future-remains-uncertain-msci-research/)). Diversification by asset-class label has been separating legal wrappers, not economic risks.

## 5. The common-factor architecture

### 5.1 Transmission channels

The correct object of measurement is not "AI weight" but the portfolio's loading on a small number of shared factors:

1. **AI capex volume** — drives semis, equipment, construction, industrial and infrastructure revenue.
2. **Compute demand and monetisation** — drives hyperscaler and model-developer cash flow, and thus lease and loan servicing.
3. **Financing conditions** — drives IG/HY spreads, ABS execution, private-credit appetite and SPV refinancing; the BIS identifies refinancing pressures, procyclical private-credit appetite and guarantee activation as explicit channels ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603u.htm)).
4. **Power availability and price** — gates delivery and determines utility/merchant economics.
5. **Asset obsolescence** — GPU and facility useful life; the IMF notes hyperscalers' implied average PP&E life of ~7 years while GPUs "could face obsolescence within two years", and shows that a three-year life assumption would cut aggregate EBIT margins by **more than 9 percentage points**, push debt from ~$800bn to over $1trn and widen debt-weighted CDS by around **60bp** ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).
6. **AI-driven disruption of incumbents** — the negative-sign channel running through software equities, leveraged loans, CLOs and private credit.
7. **Policy and social licence** — around **70% of data-centre projects face some local pushback** and New York became the first US state to impose a one-year moratorium on large new data centres ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/among-ai-crowd-some-investors-position-slower-hyperscaler-spending-growth-2026-07-17/)).

### 5.2 Circularity makes the factor more concentrated than the counterparty list suggests

The IMF documents that since 2025 "circular financing arrangements within the AI stack have become increasingly prevalent", linking listed firms (Nvidia, Oracle) with non-listed ones (OpenAI, xAI), and estimates that of a ~12 percentage point equal-weighted return gain across "AI-circle" firms from around September to end-December 2025, roughly **7 percentage points reflected correlation-reinforcement effects within the circle**, contributing about **$40bn to average market capitalisation** from a ~$2trn base. Its warning is unambiguous: circular structures create "multidirectional channels of shock transmission" that "could result in nonlinear amplification of adverse shocks", and reliance on circular finance "may amplify systemic risk by inflating valuations and masking true underlying demand" ([IMF GFSR April 2026](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)). For a pension fund this means counterparty aggregation must be done on an **economic-group basis including non-listed counterparties reached indirectly** — an LP can hold OpenAI risk through Oracle bonds, a data-centre SPV loan, a neocloud high-yield note, a VC fund and a secondaries position simultaneously.

### 5.3 2026 already delivered a live test

Three episodes show the channels co-moving:

- **February 2026 software repricing**: nearly $1trn wiped from software and services stocks following an Anthropic capability release, with software stocks −30% between October 2025 and February 2026, BDCs −10%, and high-software BDCs underperforming by ~5pp ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/global-software-stocks-hit-by-anthropic-wake-up-call-ai-disruption-2026-02-04/); [BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2603v.htm)).
- **Q1 2026 credit transmission**: software IG notes and leveraged loans traded at 89–98 cents, CLO managers moved to cut software exposure, JPMorgan estimated **$40–150bn of CLO holdings in AI-risk sectors**, and UBS put AI-disruption defaults at **3–5% versus market expectations of 1–2%** ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/debt-investors-offloading-exposure-software-companies-is-latest-sign-pain-2026-03-17/); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/software-companies-face-higher-borrowing-costs-tougher-scrutiny-ai-threatens-2026-02-23/)).
- **June–July 2026 supply indigestion**: cover ratios halved, hyperscaler and data-centre spreads widened, semis fell ~18% from their June peak while equal-weighted S&P rose 11% — the AI factor and the market factor visibly separated ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/among-ai-crowd-some-investors-position-slower-hyperscaler-spending-growth-2026-07-17/); [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/bonds/bond-issuance-backing-ai-investment-tops-250b-testing-limits-voracious-investor-demand)).

Notably, official assessments still describe risks as contained for now — the Fed's spring 2026 survey had **50% of contacts citing AI risks, up from 30% in autumn 2025**, focused on equity valuations and debt-financed capex ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20260508.pdf)), while the BoE concludes that "risks from the infrastructure investment channel are growing rapidly" ([BoE](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2026/financial-stability-report-july-2026.pdf)).

The critical framing point is that **the base case does not reduce total risk**. Broadening reduces single-name equity concentration while increasing the number of asset classes whose cash flows depend on the same capex cycle. Concentration migrates from *market-cap concentration* to *cash-flow and financing concentration*, which is less visible and harder to hedge.

### 7.1 The reporting pack

A defensible quarterly pack contains: (1) AI-weighted exposure by asset class with low/mid/high bands and confidence grades; (2) HHI and effective-N on AI-adjusted exposures; (3) top-20 ultimate economic counterparties aggregated across equity, credit, private credit, infrastructure, real estate and PE; (4) exposure by AI value-chain node and by power market; (5) AI factor beta and stress-loss contribution by sleeve; (6) flow monitor — AI share of the last four quarters' deployment versus the standing portfolio; (7) data-quality map showing what share of exposure is measured, estimated or unknown.

The headline sentence should read like this rather than a single number: *"AI-linked economic exposure is estimated at 17–32% of assets, of which X% is supported by security- or loan-level data; the dominant shared risks are named hyperscaler counterparties, data-centre demand, power availability, financing conditions and continuation of the AI capex cycle; in an AI capex-reset stress, roughly two-thirds of modelled portfolio loss arises through those shared factors."*

## 8. Implementation roadmap

1. **Weeks 1–3 — governance and taxonomy.** Ratify the three-tier taxonomy, the scoring function and weights, evidence grades, and sector caps through a cross-asset panel (equities, credit, private markets, risk). Fix the AI factor definition and the stress scenarios in advance so results cannot be negotiated afterwards.
2. **Weeks 3–6 — public markets mapping.** Score S&P 500, MSCI World/ACWI, US and global IG/HY constituents at Level 2, cross-checked against MSCI value-chain scores and at least one sell-side basket. Compute stock-versus-flow exposure for credit explicitly.
3. **Weeks 5–8 — enabling-sector attribution.** Build the utilities/industrials/materials/energy attribution model from disclosed data-centre load, contracted capacity, order backlog and rate-base plans, with caps for Grade-C priors.
4. **Weeks 6–10 — private markets look-through.** Issue the AI look-through schedule to all private managers; build the BDC-based proxy for private credit; map infrastructure and real-estate assets to offtakers, tenants and power markets; use vintage-level deployment as the leading indicator.
5. **Weeks 9–12 — aggregation, stress and reporting.** Consolidate by ultimate economic group; run scenarios; publish the pack with confidence grading; agree monitoring thresholds and escalation triggers.
6. **Ongoing.** Re-score quarterly; re-underwrite the flow monitor monthly; require new private-market commitments to be assessed for marginal contribution to AI factor exposure at investment-committee stage, not after deployment.

Two governance points deserve emphasis. First, **treat exposure limits as factor limits, not sector limits**; a cap on "technology" will be satisfied while AI exposure grows through utilities, infrastructure and private credit. Second, **require cross-manager overlap reporting**, because the S&P Global finding on LP aggregation shows that manager-level diversification and portfolio-level diversification are different things.

## 9. What would falsify the hypothesis, and what remains unknown

**Falsification tests.** The hypothesis would weaken if: (a) AI-related returns and spreads began to decorrelate across sub-sectors, e.g. power and data-centre credit holding as semiconductors sold off; (b) monetisation evidence caught up with capex, with AI revenue growth converging on capex growth as MSCI already observes in Taiwan and China; (c) financing broadened genuinely — many independent obligors rather than the same handful, with the top-20 counterparty share of AI exposure falling; or (d) the private-market AI share rose while measured cross-manager overlap fell. The July 2026 divergence between semiconductors (−18% from peak) and equal-weighted equities (+11%) is a mild point *against* full unity of the factor, and Q2 2026 earnings breadth — four of the top five S&P 500 earnings contributors outside the Magnificent Seven — cuts the same way ([FactSet](https://insight.factset.com/are-magnificent-7-companies-still-top-contributors-to-sp-500-earnings-growth-for-q2)).

**Known gaps in the evidence base.**

- **No security-level private-market dataset exists.** All private-market figures here are fundraising, deal-value or fund-universe proxies; the FSB itself highlights data gaps that hinder monitoring, including the fact that five large asset-management groups account for about one-third of aggregate loan commitments across private credit and private equity ([FSB](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P060526.pdf)).
- **Off-balance-sheet stock is only partly quantified.** Morgan Stanley's 13 April 2026 Global Fixed Income Outlook puts more than $660bn of leases and more than $640bn of disclosed data-centre purchase commitments across five hyperscalers. The 14 August 2026 US credit note adds that S&P is projecting about $585bn of debt adjustments for the four high-quality hyperscalers in 2027. Neither the BIS nor the BoE publishes a full SPV/JV census; that remains the most important missing credit data item.
- **Definitions differ materially.** "AI share of the S&P 500" ranges from 40% to 50% depending on methodology; hyperscaler capex estimates for 2026 range from about $673bn (UBS) to $725bn (BNP) to nearly $800bn (calendar-year guidance including leases), and Goldman argues hyperscaler capex both understates global AI capex by ~$200bn and overstates US AI investment by ~$200bn ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/global-investment-is-forecast-to-exceed-1-trillion-in-2026)). Any framework must publish its own definition and show sensitivity to it.
- **Useful-life assumptions are unresolved and highly material.** The gap between 7-year accounting lives and 2–4 year technology cycles determines margins, leverage, collateral values and lender recoveries simultaneously.
- **Macro backdrop is non-stationary.** The April 2026 GFSR is written against a Middle East conflict that the IMF says could slow AI investment significantly; conclusions drawn in a benign financing environment should be re-tested when spreads are not compressed.
- **Circular financing may inflate the observable data itself.** If revenues and valuations are partly recycled within the AI circle, exposure measured from reported revenue and market value is upward-biased in good times and will mean-revert faster than models assume ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf)).

## 10. Conclusion

Conventional classifications understate AI concentration for three structural reasons: they measure stocks when the risk is accumulating in flows; they classify by legal wrapper and sector label when the risk is organised by economic dependency and counterparty; and they treat asset classes as independent when data centres, power, chips, leases, loans and equity all discount the same variable — sustained AI compute demand and the financing that funds it. On current evidence, a diversified pension portfolio carries AI-linked exposure of roughly one-sixth to one-third of assets, with around two-thirds of stress loss attributable to the AI factor.

Over three to five years, the most likely path is not a reduction in concentration but a **change in its form**: from a small number of very large equity positions towards a broad set of cash-flow, financing and collateral exposures spanning credit, private credit, infrastructure and real estate. That transition will make headline equity concentration statistics look better while making the portfolio harder to de-risk, because the exposure will sit in illiquid, long-dated, contractually specified assets rather than in listed shares that can be sold in a day. The practical implication is to measure the factor, aggregate the counterparties, monitor the flows monthly, and set limits on sensitivity rather than on labels.
